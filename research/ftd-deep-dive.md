# Fails-to-Deliver: A Trading Deep Dive

*What SEC fails-to-deliver (FTD) data is, what the evidence really supports, and where — if anywhere — a short-term edge lives. Produced by a 13-agent research swarm (8 web researchers, 3 adversarial fact-checkers, 2 synthesizers); every citation was checked (46 confirmed, 5 partially corrected, 26 noted-but-unverified, 0 found false). Companion dataset: [data/ftd/](data/ftd/) (the full 2004–2026 archive, 27.55M rows). Research notes, not investment advice; nothing here is backtested.*

---

## The honest bottom line

If you take one thing from this: **the raw FTD file is a weak, noisy, ~1-month-lagged signal, and the popular "fails → imminent squeeze" story is not what the data or the peer-reviewed literature supports.** The best academic work (Fotak, Raman & Yadav 2014) associates higher fails with *higher* liquidity and pricing efficiency, not manipulation. So the realistic uses are narrow and specific:

1. **As a confirming filter, not a primary signal.** Fails add the most value stacked on top of a short-sale-constraint setup (high borrow fee + high utilization + high days-to-cover), where they corroborate that a name is genuinely hard to short — rather than as a standalone screen.
2. **The overvaluation/reversal direction, not the squeeze direction.** The documented single-name effect is that persistently high-FTD / Reg SHO threshold-list names tend to be *overvalued and subsequently underperform* (Boulton & Braga-Alves; Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves 2015) — the opposite of the retail thesis. Squeezes are a rare tail, not the base rate.
3. **Separate ETFs from single names, always.** ETFs dominate the file, and most large ETF fails are structurally benign "operational shorting" via create-to-lend (Evans, Moussawi, Pagano & Sedunov). The tradeable ETF angles are about borrow-fee carry and NAV-discount convergence, not fails per se.
4. **The lag rules out fast trades.** Publication lands ~2–4 weeks after the settlement dates it covers. Any strategy that assumes intraday or next-day reaction to a fail print is trading on look-ahead that didn't exist in real time.

The strategy catalog below is ordered by how much evidence stands behind it. Nothing here rises to "evidence-backed" — the honest ceiling on FTD signals is "plausible," and several ideas are explicitly "speculative."

### S1. Cross-Sectional High-FTD Overvaluation Short (Ex-ETF)
`plausible` · long-short (short high-FTD tail, long low-FTD tail), dollar/sector/size-neutral · 2-8 weeks, rebalanced on each semi-monthly SEC publication date

- **Thesis:** Single-name US equities carrying persistently high fails-to-deliver (scaled by shares outstanding) subsequently UNDERPERFORM. The sign is robust across studies (Stratmann-Welborn 2016 negative abnormal returns proportional to FTD level; Autore-Boulton-Braga-Alves 2015 overvaluation-then-reversal) even though the mechanism is disputed (informed shorting under nonbinding constraints vs. overvaluation under binding ones). This is the exact OPPOSITE of the retail 'high FTD = squeeze up' narrative.
- **Signal:** Parse the SEC semi-monthly pipe-delimited FTD file. POINT-IN-TIME: stamp each settlement-date row with its true publication date (first-half at ~month-end, second-half at ~the 15th of next month) and only use rows available as of the rebalance date to kill the 2-6 week look-ahead. Map CUSIP->ticker with a point-in-time crosswalk and EXCLUDE all ETFs/ETPs against an ETF master (drops the ~78% of fails that are ETF plumbing). For each single name compute FTD_intensity = open_fail_shares / shares_outstanding and dollar_fails = open_fail_shares * price / 20d_ADV. NEVER difference consecutive settlement dates to infer 'new fails' (the QUANTITY field is a cumulative open-balance snapshot, not a flow). Require persistence: FTD_intensity in the top decile across >=2 consecutive semi-monthly releases. Rank cross-sectionally and form sector- and size-neutral deciles. Short the top decile, long the bottom decile (or matched low/zero-FTD peers).
- **Instruments:** US cash equity long-short (short leg via securities lending); listed puts/put spreads on short-leg names where borrow is punitive or unavailable.
- **Entry:** On publication date, rebalance to new ranks; only include short-leg names with confirmed borrow availability and a quantified borrow fee, or route the short via defined-risk puts where borrow is punitive.
- **Exit:** Next semi-monthly rebalance; or a name's FTD_intensity leaves the top decile; or realized/expected borrow fee exceeds modeled weekly alpha; hard stop on any short that triggers a squeeze stack (utilization ~100% + CTB spiking + price breakout).
- **Sizing / risk:** Dollar-, sector-, and size-neutral; cap single-name weights ~1-2%; net-of-borrow-fee sizing (down-weight or drop names whose fee exceeds expected weekly alpha); keep gross modest given decay; budget squeeze tail explicitly on the short leg.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** If real, it is a SYMPTOM of informed short selling / overvaluation, not fails 'pushing' prices down. Who is on the other side: retail and momentum buyers of hyped, hard-to-borrow names, plus constrained arbitrageurs who cannot short cheaply and thus leave the overvaluation uncorrected.
- **Decay risks:** Documented on 2004-2008 data only; McLean-Pontiff ~58% post-publication anomaly decay; Rule 204 (2009) and T+1 settlement (May 2024) sharply cut FTD persistence and aggregate levels (GAO-09-483 ~57-80% drop); the short-leg alpha is disproportionately eaten by borrow fees (Stratmann-Welborn's profitability used PROPRIETARY borrow-cost data). Live net-of-cost edge is likely much smaller than in-sample.
- **Data needed:** SEC semi-monthly FTD file (point-in-time stamped); ETF master for exclusion; point-in-time CUSIP->ticker crosswalk + corporate-action/split adjustments; shares outstanding; price/ADV; borrow fee & availability (S3, Ortex, IHS Markit/S&P).

### S2. Reg SHO Threshold-List Overvaluation Reversal
`plausible` · short (expressed with defined-risk options on hard-to-borrow names) · weeks to ~3 months (overpricing peaks near listing and reverts thereafter)

- **Thesis:** Single names newly on / persisting on an SRO Reg SHO threshold list become significantly overvalued and then reverse (Autore-Boulton-Braga-Alves 2015), worst where short-sale constraints bind. Counterintuitively, threshold names with relatively LOW reported short interest become MORE overvalued than high-SI threshold names, because low SI signals arbitrageurs are priced out rather than absent.
- **Signal:** Use the DAILY SRO threshold lists (NYSE/Nasdaq/Cboe) as the near-real-time trigger (the SEC FTD file is too lagged for entry). Flag single-name equities that newly appear or persist >=5 settlement days; EXCLUDE ETFs and Rule 144 / convertible / shelf 'deemed-to-own' situations (their fails are benign T+35 category). Overlay valuation richness (top-quartile P/S or P/B, or a >X% 20-day run-up) and binding-constraint proxies (borrow fee >~20% annualized, utilization near 100%, thin lendable supply). Per Autore, PRIORITIZE threshold names with relatively LOW short interest. Corroborate with the SEC FTD file showing fails >=0.5% shares out across consecutive prints.
- **Instruments:** Long-dated puts / bearish put spreads / bearish risk-reversals (options are usually cheaper than borrowing the HTB stock and already price the borrow); direct cash short only where borrow is available and affordable.
- **Entry:** On/after threshold listing when valuation-rich + constraint-binding + (ideally) low SI; buy defined-risk put structures sized to survive a squeeze.
- **Exit:** Reversion toward pre-listing valuation; removal from threshold list (5 consecutive settlement days below); option expiry; or ~3-month time stop.
- **Sizing / risk:** Strictly defined-risk options to survive the squeeze tail; diversify across many threshold names; small per-name (tolerate lottery-style total loss on the ones that squeeze); never naked-short a cornered float.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Overvaluation is manufactured by binding short constraints that keep arbitrageurs out; you rent the eventual reversion via options that already embed the borrow. Who is on the other side: constrained would-be shorts and momentum/retail buyers.
- **Decay risks:** 2005-2008 sample; authors themselves call it 'difficult to arbitrage' due to extreme shorting costs; Rule 204 / T+1 shortened persistence; options IV frequently already embeds the borrow so net edge is thin; violent squeeze reversals and halts.
- **Data needed:** Daily SRO threshold lists; SEC FTD file; borrow fee / utilization / lendable supply; FINRA bi-monthly short interest; valuation fundamentals; options chain and IV.

### S3. FTD-Confirmed Short-Constraint Long-Short
`plausible` · long-short, dollar/sector-neutral · ~1 month rebalance

- **Thesis:** FTD level alone is a weak, noisy, lagged signal. Used ONLY as a confirming filter on a short-sale-constraint anomaly (high borrow fee + high utilization + high SI), FTD presence corroborates that the constraint is 'real' (fails accompany genuinely scarce borrow), selecting the constrained/overvalued names to short against matched unconstrained peers.
- **Signal:** Build a real-time short-constraint composite from vendor data: borrow_fee percentile, utilization (>90%), SI%float, days-to-cover. Apply an FTD GATE from the SEC file: fails >=0.5% shares out AND top-quintile FTD_intensity persisting across >=2 semi-monthly prints, ex-ETF, point-in-time. Only names passing BOTH the constraint composite AND the FTD gate enter the short book; long a matched basket (same sector/size) of unconstrained, low/zero-FTD peers.
- **Instruments:** Long-short US cash equity; puts on the most punitive-to-borrow shorts.
- **Entry:** Monthly, aligned to FTD publication, when both gates pass and borrow is quantified.
- **Exit:** Gate failure (borrow normalizes or FTD clears); 1-month rebalance; or squeeze stop on the short leg.
- **Sizing / risk:** Dollar/sector-neutral; explicit borrow-carry budget; cap concentration; keep gross modest because these are the highest-fee, lowest-liquidity names.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Combining two individually weak signals raises precision; FTD confirms the borrow constraint is economically binding rather than a transient quote. Who is on the other side: retail longs and arbitrageurs priced out by borrow cost.
- **Decay risks:** Same borrow-cost drag on the short leg; both inputs are regime-shifted/decayed; heavy multicollinearity (FTD, fee, utilization, SI all proxy the same latent short-constraint), so FTD may add little INCREMENTAL information over borrow-fee data alone.
- **Data needed:** SEC FTD (PIT, ex-ETF); borrow fee/utilization/lendable (S3/Ortex/Markit); FINRA short interest; ADV; sector/size classifications.

### S4. Fade the FTD/MOASS Squeeze Blow-Off
`plausible` · short (via defined-risk options only) · days to a few weeks, entered post blow-off

- **Thesis:** Viral 'T+35 cycle / FTD spike -> imminent squeeze' chatter around a parabolic, heavily-promoted low-float name, where published fails are elevated on one print but NOT persistent and there is no genuinely cornered float, usually does NOT produce a squeeze and mean-reverts. The GME staff report (Oct 18, 2021) found positive sentiment (not buy-to-cover) sustained that run; Fotak et al. find fails are largely benign. You sell the squeeze narrative back to retail.
- **Signal:** Identify low-float names with (a) a spike in social/retail squeeze chatter or a widely publicized threshold-list appearance, (b) a parabolic 5-20 day run-up, and (c) FTD data that is elevated on one print but NOT persistent across consecutive semi-monthly releases (fails self-cured under Rule 204). Confirm the squeeze fuel is ABSENT: SI not extreme, borrow available, days-to-cover low. Treat any 'T+35 calendar count' as noise, not signal (evidence contradicts a mechanical T+35 edge; T+35 is a narrow deemed-to-own provision, not the naked-short clock). Enter only AFTER a blow-off / lower-high confirms exhaustion.
- **Instruments:** Defined-risk long puts / put spreads on the hyped equity. Avoid outright short stock (uncapped tail).
- **Entry:** After a clear parabolic exhaustion or lower-high following the hype peak; buy puts/put spreads sized to survive a further squeeze leg.
- **Exit:** Reversion toward the pre-hype range; option time-decay limit; or stop-and-invalidate on a fresh breakout to new highs.
- **Sizing / risk:** Strictly long-premium / defined-risk; small per-name; expect a low hit rate with occasional total loss on the name that does squeeze; diversify across many independent fades.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** The squeeze narrative is empirically weak and the fails are transient/benign; retail overpays for the dream. Who is on the other side: retail momentum buyers ('apes') buying the squeeze thesis and gamma.
- **Decay risks:** Events are violent and idiosyncratic - one true squeeze can erase multiple wins; social dynamics can extend blow-offs; IV is extremely rich so puts are expensive; halts/delistings; requires disciplined post-exhaustion timing.
- **Data needed:** SEC FTD (persistence check across prints); social-sentiment feed (Reddit/StockTwits/X mention counts); float, SI, borrow fee, days-to-cover; options chain / IV; price/volume.

### S5. Option-Implied Borrow vs. Loan-Fee Divergence
`plausible` · long-short / arbitrage-conditional · intraday to days (out to option expiry)

- **Thesis:** In hard-to-borrow optionable names, a major options market maker often rationally FAILS rather than borrows (Evans, Geczy, Musto & Reed 2009), so the option-implied cost of a synthetic short (from put-call parity / the conversion-reversal box) embeds the LEVEL of the effective borrow/fail cost and can diverge from the quoted stock-loan fee. Route a desired short into whichever leg is cheaper; run a real conversion only with genuine borrow access.
- **Signal:** For each HTB optionable name, back out the option-implied borrow rate from put-call parity on matched strikes/expiries (reversal-conversion box: solve for the borrow/dividend-adjusted forward embedded in call - put - (S - K*e^-rT)), averaging several near-the-money strikes to reduce noise. Compute signal = implied_borrow - quoted_stock_loan_fee. A large gap means the synthetic short is cheap (or rich) relative to shares. Corroborate HTB status with SEC FTD presence, high utilization, and threshold-list membership. CRITICAL (Muravyev-Pearson-Pollet 2022): option prices track the LEVEL of the loan fee, but the borrowing-fee RISK premium is small and does NOT predict fee-adjusted returns - so trade this as a transient level/basis dislocation and cheaper-leg selection, NOT as harvesting a lending-fee risk premium.
- **Instruments:** Listed options reversals/conversions and box spreads versus actual securities borrow; or simply route the intended short into the cheaper of synthetic-via-options vs. cash short.
- **Entry:** When |implied_borrow - quoted_fee| exceeds transaction costs plus a margin AND you have genuine borrow access to run the real conversion; otherwise use the divergence as a risk/HTB flag only.
- **Exit:** Basis convergence; option expiry; borrow recall; or when carry erodes the edge.
- **Sizing / risk:** Institutional, capacity-constrained; needs cheap borrow and low option transaction costs; early-assignment / pin risk on option legs; margin for the box; size to actual borrow availability.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Segmentation between the stock-loan market and the options market in HTB names; 'failing is cheaper than borrowing' leaks value into option prices. Who is on the other side: options MMs who fail rather than borrow, and stock-loan desks quoting a different fee.
- **Decay risks:** Largely arbitraged by institutions; the 2008 removal of the OPTIONS market-maker CLOSE-OUT exception (Release 34-58775) reduced the 'fail instead of borrow' wedge; T+1 compresses the window; retail cannot access the borrow to arbitrage (flag-only). Per Muravyev, there is no risk premium to harvest, only a transient level basis.
- **Data needed:** Full options chain (bid/ask by matched strike/expiry), dividend forecasts, risk-free curve; quoted stock-loan fee & availability; SEC FTD / threshold list / utilization for HTB corroboration.
- **⚠️ Legality:** A bona-fide reversal/conversion run against a genuine locate/borrow is legal relative-value; it must NOT be structured as a deep-ITM buy-write / married-put 'reset' device that fakes a Rule 204 close-out (see the detection strategy). Keep locate logs and order marks clean.

### S6. Illiquid-Underlying ETF NAV-Discount Convergence
`plausible` · long (discount-to-NAV convergence, underlying-hedged) · days to weeks

- **Thesis:** When a liquid ETF holding illiquid assets (corporate/HY/muni/EM/bank-loan bonds) trades at a wide, persistent discount to NAV during funding stress, AP arbitrage is impaired by the AP's dealer/arbitrageur inventory conflict (Pan-Zeng liquidity mismatch; the March 2020 dash-for-cash saw AGG ~-4.4%, BND ~-6.2%, LQD ~-4.5% on 16 Mar 2020), and ETF FTDs spike as APs fail on operational shorts. If arbitrage or a policy backstop restores the link, the discount converges.
- **Signal:** Monitor ETF price vs. NAV/iNAV for bond ETFs (LQD, HYG, AGG, BND, muni, EM-debt, bank-loan). Trigger when the discount exceeds a stress threshold (e.g., > 2-4% and widening) AND coincides with (a) a spike in that ETF's FTDs in the SEC file or a threshold-list appearance (corroborating that AP arbitrage is jammed as operational shorts fail) and (b) broad funding-stress indicators (widening credit spreads, cross-currency basis, elevated MOVE). Enter a convergence trade long the ETF hedged against the underlying.
- **Instruments:** Long the ETF vs. short a NAV proxy (constituent basket / index futures / a liquid sister ETF), or long ETF calls; pairs vs. futures.
- **Entry:** At an extreme discount with FTD/threshold corroboration during confirmed funding stress; scale in.
- **Exit:** Discount converges toward NAV; policy backstop announced; or stop if the 'discount' turns out to be true underlying repricing (stale NAV) and widens further.
- **Sizing / risk:** Hedge the ETF's rate/credit beta so you isolate the basis; size for the possibility convergence needs a policy backstop; keep dry powder to average as discounts can widen violently before reverting.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** A temporary breakdown of AP arbitrage under inventory conflict; you supply the liquidity APs cannot. Who is on the other side: forced ETF sellers dashing for cash and inventory-constrained APs. But you are effectively short the option that the backstop never comes.
- **Decay risks:** Rare (needs systemic stress); end-of-day NAV can be stale/model-priced, so part of the 'discount' is genuine repricing not yet in NAV; in March 2020 the gap only closed after the Fed's SMCCF - convergence can require policy, not patience - and backstop timing is unknowable.
- **Data needed:** ETF price, NAV/iNAV and its marking/staleness method; SEC FTD / threshold list for the ETF; funding-stress indicators; futures/basket for the hedge.

### S7. Create-to-Lend Borrow-Fee Harvest (XRT-Type ETFs)
`plausible` · long (carry; market/sector-neutral once hedged) · weeks to months

- **Thesis:** Persistently hard-to-borrow ETFs (XRT and similar sector ETFs whose SI has repeatedly exceeded 100% of shares outstanding) sustain a high cost-to-borrow while 'create-to-lend' keeps supply elastic, so an owner can hold the ETF and lend it out to harvest the borrow fee as CARRY. This is explicitly NOT a squeeze bet - APs freely create shares, so SI>100% here is benign plumbing, not a cornered float.
- **Signal:** Screen ETFs with sustained high CTB (e.g., > 5-20% annualized and stable), frequent Reg SHO threshold-list appearance, and SI > 100% of shares outstanding, where create-to-lend is active (borrow remains available despite the high fee - the tell that supply is elastic). SEC FTD / threshold data confirm the operational-shorting demand that drives the lending fee. Long the ETF, enroll shares in a securities-lending program, and hedge the ETF's market/sector beta with futures or a sister ETF to isolate the carry.
- **Instruments:** Long the ETF (or its constituent basket) + securities-lending program; beta hedge via sector futures or a correlated sister ETF.
- **Entry:** When CTB is high and stable and lending demand persists; put on the beta hedge simultaneously.
- **Exit:** CTB collapses (demand fades or create-to-lend floods supply); the hedge basis blows out; or lending recall.
- **Sizing / risk:** Beta MUST be hedged (the fee is the entire edge); manage recall and hedge tracking error; do not up-size expecting a squeeze - treat purely as decaying carry.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** You supply lendable inventory into structurally short-heavy sector ETFs; create-to-lend means the fee is a real, recurring borrow demand, not a squeeze. Who is on the other side: operational shorts and 'backdoor short' sellers borrowing the ETF as a basket hedge.
- **Decay risks:** Elastic create-to-lend supply means the fee mean-reverts fast; competition from other lenders and from fresh AP creation compresses it; recall risk; the whole P&L is the fee, so any beta-hedge tracking error can swamp it.
- **Data needed:** ETF borrow fee/utilization/lendable supply; SI; SEC FTD / threshold list; ETF NAV/tracking error; access to a securities-lending program; hedge instrument.

### S8. Daily Short-Volume Reversal (FINRA) with FTD Gate
`plausible` · long-short, dollar/sector-neutral · ~1-4 weeks (~20 trading days)

- **Thesis:** Daily short-selling flow predicts negative abnormal returns over ~20 trading days (Diether-Lee-Werner 2009): short the most heavily short-sold names and long the least, cross-sectionally. FTD data is added only as a SLOW confirming gate (its multi-week lag makes it useless as the trigger), tightening the short book toward names where fails corroborate genuine, borrow-constrained short demand.
- **Signal:** From FINRA daily short-sale volume (public, near-real-time), compute short_ratio = short_volume / total_volume per name; rank cross-sectionally, sector/size-neutral. Short the high-short_ratio decile, long the low decile. Apply an FTD GATE on the SHORT leg: where the (lagged) SEC FTD file already shows fails in the top quintile / >=0.5% shares out, keep/overweight; where FTD is absent, treat the short-volume signal as more likely plus-tick/MM noise and down-weight. Exclude ETFs throughout.
- **Instruments:** US cash equity long-short.
- **Entry:** Daily or weekly rebalance on the short-volume ranks; apply the FTD gate where FTD data is available.
- **Exit:** Rank decay; ~20-day horizon; or stop.
- **Sizing / risk:** Dollar/sector-neutral; account for higher turnover and short-leg borrow cost; keep gross moderate given decay and cost sensitivity.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Short-sale flow carries information (informed shorting), and FTD presence confirms borrow-constrained conviction rather than benign market-making. Who is on the other side: liquidity-demanding and uninformed momentum buyers.
- **Decay risks:** Well-known and published (2009) so likely decayed; FINRA short-volume mixes MM/hedging flow (not all 'informed'); the FTD gate is stale by construction and may add little; high turnover makes it transaction-cost sensitive.
- **Data needed:** FINRA daily short-sale volume files; SEC FTD (point-in-time gate, ex-ETF); ETF master; borrow fee; price/volume.

### S9. Ex-Dividend FTD De-Noising & Assignment-Risk Filter
`plausible` · filter / avoid (optional long-carry via lending) · 1-5 days around each ex-dividend date

- **Thesis:** In hard-to-borrow dividend payers, deep-ITM call OI is large and FTD/short-selling data spike around ex-dividend dates due to dividend-capture reversals and irrational non-exercise (Hao-Kalay-Mayhew 2010), then revert within days. These transient, benign FTD spikes must be excluded from single-name FTD signals to avoid false positives; separately, do not write deep-ITM calls into the dividend crowd or carry shorts over ex-date.
- **Signal:** Maintain an ex-dividend calendar for HTB dividend payers. Tag any FTD observation whose settlement date falls in the [ex-date - 2, ex-date + 3] window as dividend-arb-driven and EXCLUDE / down-weight it from the overvaluation, squeeze, and detection screens (this filter feeds the other strategies). Detect the pattern via deep-ITM call OI concentration plus FTD/short spikes clustered on the day before/at ex-date that revert within days. Use it as (a) a data-hygiene filter and (b) a trade-avoidance rule: do not initiate shorts to carry over ex-date in HTB names, and do not write deep-ITM calls the dividend crowd will exercise/assign.
- **Instruments:** N/A as standalone alpha - a filter and risk-avoidance rule layered on the other strategies; optionally an owned-share lending overlay into the pre-ex fee spike (carry).
- **Entry:** N/A for the filter; for the optional overlay, lend owned HTB dividend-payer shares into the pre-ex borrow-fee spike.
- **Exit:** The FTD/fee spike reverts within days after ex-date; unwind any lending overlay as the fee normalizes.
- **Sizing / risk:** Minimal as a filter; the lending overlay carries recall/assignment considerations; strictly avoid holding written deep-ITM calls over ex-date.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Correctly attributing benign, calendar-driven fails avoids false manipulation/overvaluation flags in the other strategies; the dividend option game itself is dominated by low-cost market makers, so retail edge here is avoidance, not alpha. Who is on the other side (for the lending overlay): dividend-capture arbitrageurs borrowing the shares.
- **Decay risks:** Low decay as a hygiene/avoidance rule, but its value is entirely derivative of the primary strategies it filters; the lending overlay's fee is transient and small.
- **Data needed:** Ex-dividend calendar; SEC FTD (settlement-date tagging); options chain (deep-ITM call OI); borrow fee.

### S10. HTB Multi-Factor Squeeze Long (Tail Lottery)
`speculative` · long · days to a couple of weeks

- **Thesis:** Rarely, the full constraint stack - elevated persistent FTD + utilization ~100% + spiking cost-to-borrow + high days-to-cover + price already turning up on rising volume - precedes a genuine short squeeze driven by Rule 204 forced close-out / pre-borrow ('penalty box') demand into a thin float. This is a low-probability, positive-skew TAIL bet, explicitly NOT a base-rate edge (the dominant evidence is that high-FTD names underperform).
- **Signal:** Single names only (ex-ETF, ex-Rule-144). Require ALL of: FTD_intensity elevated and persistent in the SEC file; utilization >=95%; CTB spiking (>50% annualized and rising week-on-week); days-to-cover >5; AND price momentum turning up (20-day higher-low on rising volume). Treat the lagged FTD file as the slow corroborating leg; the real-time triggers are borrow-desk/loan-market stress and price/volume confirmation. Do NOT enter without the price upturn (avoid catching a falling knife against the dominant bearish effect).
- **Instruments:** Long call spreads (defined risk) preferred; small long equity as an alternative.
- **Entry:** When every factor aligns AND price confirms an upturn; buy call spreads to cap premium outlay.
- **Exit:** Momentum break; borrow-fee collapse (squeeze resolving); fixed profit multiple (e.g., 2-3x on the structure); or short time stop.
- **Sizing / risk:** Lottery-ticket sizing (tiny per name, strictly defined risk); expect most to expire worthless; only justified by positive skew, never as a core position.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** Mechanical Rule 204 close-out plus pre-borrow demand can force covering into a cornered float. Who is on the other side: trapped shorts and brokers forced to buy-in / pre-borrow. But the base rate is against you.
- **Decay risks:** You are fighting the dominant effect (high-FTD names fall on average); genuine squeezes are rare; borrow can be recalled; halts/delistings; IV extremely rich; T+1 settlement resolves fails faster, shrinking the persistent-fail fuel that historically powered squeezes.
- **Data needed:** Real-time borrow fee/utilization/days-to-cover (S3/Ortex); SEC FTD (persistence); float/SI; price/volume; options chain.

### S11. Reset-Transaction Detection Overlay (Sham Close-Out Flag)
`speculative` · short-bias / avoid-long · weeks (as long as the reset pattern persists)

- **Thesis:** Fails that stay elevated across multiple prints despite the compressed T+1 Rule 204 close-out are a red flag that a close-out obligation is being artificially RESET via deep-ITM buy-writes / married puts / reverse conversions (per SEC v. optionsXpress 2012; SEC v. Hazan/TJM 2009; OCIE Risk Alert Aug 9, 2013). Detecting the pattern flags names where the 'fail' is being maintained rather than genuinely resolved - a bearish/avoid tilt on the underlying and a warning that public FTD numbers may be masked.
- **Signal:** Combine (a) SEC FTD data showing fails that remain elevated (>=0.5% shares out) across multiple consecutive semi-monthly prints despite the Rule 204 close-out that should clear them, with (b) options-market fingerprints: clusters of deep-in-the-money buy-write volume, abnormal OI in very short-dated / 1-day FLEX or deep-ITM listed calls, and OI/volume spikes concentrated around settlement/close-out dates. Cross-reference the OCIE 2013 Risk Alert red-flag list. Explicitly separate benign look-alikes: legitimate dividend arbitrage, hedging, and bona-fide market making produce similar footprints - correlation is not proof, and CNS-netted public data cannot attribute a fail to any account.
- **Instruments:** Small defined-risk bearish options on the flagged underlying and/or exclusion of the name from any long/squeeze book; primarily a surveillance / data-quality filter, secondarily a mild short tilt.
- **Entry:** When persistent-fail AND the deep-ITM reset fingerprint are both present; express as small defined-risk bearish exposure or as a hard exclusion from long/squeeze baskets.
- **Exit:** Pattern clears (fails resolve and deep-ITM clusters stop); regulatory action; or thesis invalidation.
- **Sizing / risk:** Small, defined-risk; treat as a filter more than a position; real legal/headline risk if publicizing manipulation accusations.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** The reset is a legal-cost arbitrage by whoever maintains the short; an artificially-maintained fail signals an unresolved bearish position and a possible eventual forced unwind or enforcement action. Who is on the other side: the broker/customer running the resets and their assignees.
- **Decay risks:** Post-2008/2009 rules plus FINRA exam focus (2023-2025) actively suppress this; genuine reset abuse is rarer and harder to see from public data; high false-positive rate from legitimate dividend arb; you cannot attribute fails to an account from public data, so flags are hypotheses only.
- **Data needed:** SEC FTD (persistence across prints); full options chain with OI/volume by strike/expiry and FLEX activity; ex-dividend calendar; borrow/threshold data; ideally OPRA time-and-sales to detect buy-write pairs.
- **⚠️ Legality:** Executing sham reset transactions, faking locates, or falsely claiming bona-fide market-making to evade Reg SHO Rules 203/204 is ILLEGAL (and, where deception is proven, violates Section 10(b)/17(a)). This is a DETECTION/surveillance strategy that trades only the public underlying/options on the inference - it never executes resets. Public CNS-netted FTD data cannot attribute fails to any account; treat all flags as hypotheses, not proof of manipulation, and do not publish unproven accusations.

### S12. ETF Operational-Shorting FTD Risk Overlay
`speculative` · risk-off overlay (long protection) · regime / risk-management (weeks to months)

- **Thesis:** A sustained, broad rise in ETF operational-shorting FTDs - especially in illiquid-underlying ETFs - proxies elevated authorized-participant counterparty / systemic risk (Evans, Moussawi, Pagano & Sedunov). This is NOT directional alpha; it is a regime-level risk overlay to widen hedges, cut gross, or buy tail protection on affected ETFs/sectors.
- **Signal:** Aggregate ETF FTDs from the SEC file over time (ETF subset only), tracking the level and BREADTH of ETF fails and their concentration by underlying-liquidity bucket (corporate-bond, HY, EM, bank-loan ETFs are most fragile). Because firm/AP-level CNS data is confidential, proxy concentration via which ETFs/sponsors show correlated FTD spikes and threshold-list clustering. Flag a risk-off regime when this ETF-FTD stress indicator crosses a high historical percentile alongside independent funding-stress signals.
- **Instruments:** Risk overlay - index/sector tail hedges (puts), reduced gross exposure, tail protection on affected bond ETFs. Not a standalone entry.
- **Entry:** When the ETF-FTD stress indicator crosses a high percentile AND corroborating funding-stress indicators fire (gate to cut false alarms).
- **Exit:** Normalization of ETF FTDs and funding indicators.
- **Sizing / risk:** Budget the carry cost of tail hedges; gate strictly on corroborating stress to avoid bleeding on false alarms; never over-hedge on FTD alone.
- **Why an edge (and who's on the other side):** ETF operational shorting concentrates counterparty/plumbing risk that is invisible in price until stress hits; the overlay is insurance, not alpha. Who is on the other side: you are buying protection, so effectively the vol sellers.
- **Decay risks:** ETF fails are USUALLY benign and self-curing, so most spikes never presage stress (high false-positive rate); the SEC file is semi-monthly and lagged; hard to calibrate a threshold that is timely and not noisy.
- **Data needed:** SEC FTD file (ETF subset, time series); ETF/sponsor mapping (AP concentration proxied, since firm-level CNS data is confidential); funding-stress indicators; options for hedges.

## Backtesting FTD signals without fooling yourself

The data's structure — a biweekly, ~1-month-lagged snapshot of *cumulative open* fails per CUSIP, aggregated across participants — creates specific traps. A credible backtest has to be built around them.

**Methodology**

1. **1. Point-in-time (PIT) availability stamping — the anti-look-ahead spine** — Each row carries a SETTLEMENT DATE but the file itself carries NO publication date, so reconstruct a true `pit_available_date` per file: first-half files (…a / settlement days ~1–15) are posted around month-end of the SAME month; second-half files (…b / settlement days ~16–EOM) are posted around the 15th of the FOLLOWING month. Because the SEC explicitly does not guarantee posting dates, add a conservative buffer (e.g., first business day after the nominal post, or empirically calibrate from Wayback/EDGAR archive timestamps). Every signal for a given settlement date may only enter a portfolio on or after its pit_available_date. This yields a ~2–6 week enforced lag and is the single most important control.
2. **2. Raw ingest and schema normalization** — Parse the pipe-delimited layout (SETTLEMENT DATE|CUSIP|SYMBOL|QUANTITY (FAILS)|DESCRIPTION|PRICE). Strip the two trailer records ('Trailer record count', 'Trailer total quantity of shares'); the manifest's trailer_count/data_rows lets you assert row integrity. Coerce SETTLEMENT DATE→date, QUANTITY→int64, PRICE '.'→NULL (missing or sub-penny). Tag each row with file_id, half-month period, and the manifest min/max settlement to detect gaps.
3. **3. Regime segmentation (enforce stationarity boundaries)** — Mark each row's regime from its settlement date: the Sept 16, 2008 reporting-floor change (pre: only balances ≥10,000 shares are reported; post: ALL open balances) is a hard break — never pool cross-sectional distributions across it. Also flag Rule 204T (Sep 2008)/final Rule 204 (Jul 2009), options-MM close-out exception removal (Oct 2008), T+3→T+2 (Sep 2017), and T+2→T+1 (May 28, 2024). Use these as sub-sample cuts and regime dummies; assume fail persistence structurally declined after each close-out tightening.
4. **4. Security-type classification and universe definition** — Classify every CUSIP into single-name US common equity vs ETF/ETP vs ADR/foreign (the file is full of them — Mercedes, TotalEnergies, CMB.TECH) vs closed-end fund vs unit/warrant/right vs Rule-144 restricted. Hard-exclude ETFs (they are ~78% of all fails and reflect benign AP operational shorting, not single-name short pressure) and analyze them only as a separate mechanics/risk overlay. Decide explicitly on ADRs (exclude or segment — their fails reflect cross-border settlement). Investable universe = US-listed common stock passing price (>$1–5), ADV, market-cap, and borrowability floors, defined POINT-IN-TIME.
5. **5. Point-in-time CUSIP↔security-master crosswalk and corporate-action handling** — Map CUSIP to a permanent security id (e.g., CRSP PERMNO) AS-OF the settlement date, because CUSIPs get reassigned and tickers recycled. Carry the mapping's as-of validity so a later reuse of a ticker/CUSIP cannot leak. QUANTITY is RAW shares — split-adjust it onto the same adjustment basis as the return series before any ratio. Reconcile against the DESCRIPTION field; drop unmappable rows and report the drop rate.
6. **6. Signal construction — treat the series as a LEVEL, never a flow** — QUANTITY is a snapshot of the cumulative open-fail balance, so NEVER difference consecutive settlement dates to get 'new fails' and NEVER sum the series over time (the SEC's own dominant-misuse warning). Build: (a) fail INTENSITY = fails/shares-outstanding and fails/free-float, plus dollar-fails = fails×price / ADV; (b) PERSISTENCE = count of consecutive published prints above a threshold, or fraction of settlement days in the window with balance>X; (c) an SRO threshold-list membership flag as a separate, less-lagged signal. All computed on PIT snapshots.
7. **7. Cross-sectional normalization and tail control** — Within each PIT universe, cross-sectionally rank or z-score the signal, log-transform the heavy right tail, and winsorize (e.g., 1/99%). Normalize by free float (not just shares outstanding) so corporate structure is controlled, but guard the tiny-float denominator: apply an illiquidity/float floor BEFORE dividing so micro-float names cannot produce runaway intensity values that dominate the sort.
8. **8. Confound controls and neutralization** — The central identification problem: high-FTD names are mechanically the hardest and most expensive to borrow, so raw 'FTD alpha' can just be the borrow/short-interest anomaly relabeled. Control for and double-sort on size (log mktcap), liquidity (ADV, Amihud, spread), short interest (%float, days-to-cover), borrow fee and utilization, momentum, value, idiosyncratic vol, and an optionable/ETF flag. Use Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional regressions to isolate the INCREMENTAL predictive coefficient on FTD intensity beyond SI and borrow, and report the borrow-orthogonalized signal.
9. **9. Benchmark, factor-neutralization, and dual design (cross-sectional AND event-study)** — Cross-sectional: form decile/quintile portfolios on the PIT signal, rebalance on each pit_available_date (semi-monthly), size/sector-neutral; measure alpha vs FF5+MOM plus a liquidity factor (Pastor-Stambaugh) and DGTW characteristic-matched abnormal returns. Event-study: define events as first appearance / threshold-crossing of FTD intensity (or threshold-list add), then use CALENDAR-TIME portfolios (not naive event-window averaging) to handle event clustering/cross-correlation, benchmarked to matched controls over horizons the literature implies (~1–8 weeks up to ~3 months). Prior evidence is bearish-on-average for high-FTD single names (overvaluation reversal / informed shorting), so the base-case hypothesis is fade-the-high-FTD-tail, NOT the retail squeeze thesis.
10. **10. Cost model and tradeability gate** — Model the short leg's borrow fee explicitly (it disproportionately hits exactly the high-FTD/HTB names and can eat most of the paper alpha), plus commissions, slippage/impact scaled by 1/ADV, and semi-monthly rebalance turnover. Apply a 'can this actually be shorted, and at what fee' filter; where borrow is punitive or unavailable, re-express the short via listed puts and price the option's embedded borrow. Net-of-cost is the headline result; gross is diagnostic only.
11. **11. Multiple-testing discipline, OOS, and decay accounting** — Pre-register the hypothesis set and log every specification tried (normalizations × horizons × controls × regimes). Apply deflated/probabilistic Sharpe and BHY-FDR / Holm corrections for the trial count. Require sign-stability, decile monotonicity, and robustness across the regime cuts, plus walk-forward / true out-of-sample validation. Explicitly discount external validity: the canonical results are 2005–2008 (pre-Rule 204, pre-T+1), anomalies decay post-publication (McLean-Pontiff), and close-out tightening likely shrank the live edge.

**Pitfalls → mitigations**

- **Look-ahead from the publication lag: using the SETTLEMENT date as if it were the trade date, capturing ~2–6 weeks of unavailable information.** → Reconstruct and enforce a per-file pit_available_date (first-half≈month-end, second-half≈15th of next month) plus a conservative buffer; portfolios may only act on/after that date; validate the mapping against archived snapshots.
- **Snapshot-vs-flow misuse: the QUANTITY field is a cumulative OPEN-fail balance, so differencing consecutive prints to get 'new fails' or summing the series over time is invalid (the SEC's own flagged dominant error).** → Treat QUANTITY strictly as a level; build intensity and persistence features from levels only; never difference or cumulate the raw series.
- **2008 reporting-floor discontinuity: pre-Sept-16-2008 files report only balances ≥10,000 shares (visible as the ~70k→~145k row-count jump), left-censoring small fails and creating a spurious level break.** → Regime-split at 2008-09-16; never pool distributions across it; when a continuous long series is required, re-impose the 10,000-share floor on post-2008 data or analyze eras separately.
- **CUSIP/ticker and corporate-action errors: reassigned CUSIPs, recycled tickers, and un-split-adjusted raw share counts silently corrupt ratios and can leak future identity; vendor mappings propagate their own errors.** → Use a point-in-time CUSIP→permanent-id security master with as-of validity; split-adjust QUANTITY onto the return-series basis; reconcile against DESCRIPTION; drop and quantify unmappable rows.
- **ETF vs single-stock pooling: ETFs are ~10% of market cap but ~78% of fails via benign operational shorting (AP create-to-cure), swamping any single-name signal.** → Hard-exclude ETFs/ETPs from the single-name cross-section via the security-type classifier; treat ETF fails only as a separate liquidity/systemic-risk overlay, never as directional single-name alpha.
- **Survivorship and delisting bias: the strategy shorts distressed/overvalued names that halt, delist, or go bankrupt; a survivor-only universe or dropping delisted names inflates returns and mis-handles the short-leg payoff.** → Use a delisting-inclusive security master; assign proper CRSP delisting returns to the short leg; retain halted/bankrupt names through their terminal return.
- **Multiple testing / data snooping across many normalizations, horizons, controls, and regimes inflates false discoveries.** → Pre-register hypotheses and count all trials; apply deflated/probabilistic Sharpe and FDR (BHY)/Holm corrections; require decile monotonicity, sign-stability across regimes, and out-of-sample/walk-forward confirmation.
- **Tiny-float illiquidity and non-tradeability: the signal concentrates in micro-caps where fails/float explodes, borrow is unavailable or punitive, and market impact is severe — paper alpha that cannot be arbitraged.** → Apply price/ADV/market-cap/borrowability floors and float-floor-before-dividing; winsorize normalized intensity; deduct realistic borrow fees; cap positions by ADV; express shorts via puts where cash-borrow is impossible.
- **ADR/foreign/CINS contamination: the file contains many non-US issuers (Mercedes, TotalEnergies, CMB.TECH) whose fails reflect cross-border settlement mechanics, not domestic short pressure.** → Classify by country/CINS and either exclude ADRs/foreign issuers or model them as a separate segment with their own settlement dynamics.
- **Confounding with short interest and borrow cost: high-FTD names ARE the expensive-to-borrow names, so 'FTD alpha' may be the borrow/SI anomaly relabeled rather than incremental information.** → Double-sort and control on short interest, borrow fee, and utilization; use Fama-MacBeth to isolate the incremental FTD coefficient; report a borrow-orthogonalized signal and compare to a pure-borrow benchmark.
- **Reset/masking and forced close-out: sham reset transactions (optionsXpress-style) and post-2009 Rule 204 buy-ins cure or disguise most fails BEFORE publication, so the published level understates and destabilizes the true short-pressure it proxies.** → Treat the published balance as a lagged lower bound, not ground truth; corroborate with real-time borrow fee/utilization/short interest; expect signal weakening and build in decay assumptions.
- **Regime non-stationarity: Rule 204 (2009) and the move to T+2 (2017) then T+1 (2024) progressively compressed fail persistence, so a backtest spanning the breaks mixes structurally different distributions and over-credits pre-2009 behavior.** → Use regime dummies and sub-period sub-samples; walk-forward validate; heavily discount the external validity of 2005–2008 magnitudes for the current regime.

**Evaluation metrics**

- Net-of-cost long-short Sharpe / information ratio with borrow fee explicitly deducted on the short leg (report gross only as a diagnostic)
- Factor alpha and t-stat vs FF5+MOM plus a Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity factor, and DGTW characteristic-matched abnormal return
- Fama-MacBeth incremental coefficient and t-stat on FTD intensity after controlling for size, liquidity, short interest, and borrow cost (the key identification metric)
- Event-study CARs/BHARs evaluated via calendar-time portfolio t-stats across 1-week / 2-week / 1-month / 3-month horizons
- Decile monotonicity of returns in the signal (top-minus-bottom spread plus rank correlation across all deciles)
- Deflated / probabilistic Sharpe ratio and FDR-adjusted p-values accounting for the number of specifications tested
- Turnover, average holding period, and estimated capacity (AUM at which market impact erodes the net alpha)
- Breadth per rebalance: number of investable names, fraction of the universe actually shortable, and average borrow fee of the short leg
- Regime-conditional performance across the four eras (pre-2008 floor, 2009–2017, 2017–2024, post-T+1) and the out-of-sample-vs-in-sample decay ratio
- Tail-risk diagnostics on the short leg: skew/kurtosis, worst single-name contribution, short-squeeze drawdown, and left-tail VaR/expected shortfall

**Concrete data pipeline**

1. Ingest all 426 archives from raw/ and extracted/ driven by manifest.csv (zip,txt,data_rows,min_settlement,max_settlement,trailer_count); parse the pipe-delimited layout, strip the two trailer records, assert data_rows==parsed==trailer_count, coerce QUANTITY→int64 and PRICE '.'→NULL.
2. Derive per-file metadata: file_id, half-month period (…a first-half / …b second-half), and a reconstructed pit_available_date (first-half≈same-month-end, second-half≈15th-of-next-month) plus a conservative posting buffer; store as the enforced trade-eligibility timestamp.
3. Stamp each row's regime from its settlement date: pre/post 2008-09-16 reporting floor, Rule 204T/204, OMM-exception removal, T+2 (2017-09-05), T+1 (2024-05-28).
4. Build/join a point-in-time security master keyed on CUSIP→permanent id with as-of validity, security type (common/ETF/ADR/CEF/unit/warrant/restricted), country/CINS flag, shares outstanding, free float, split factors, and an optionable flag.
5. Consolidate into a partitioned parquet (by year and half-month period) that replaces the placeholder parquet/ftd_all.parquet, with columns: settlement_date, pit_available_date, cusip, permid, symbol, sec_type, country, qty_fails, qty_fails_split_adj, price, shares_out, float, regime.
6. Join external panels as-of the correct lag: adjusted price/return, ADV, market cap, spread/Amihud (settlement date); short interest (bi-monthly, own publication lag); borrow fee and utilization (vendor); FF/DGTW factor returns; delisting returns.
7. Compute PIT signals strictly from levels: fails/shares_out, fails/float, dollar-fails/ADV, and persistence counts (consecutive prints above threshold); winsorize, log-transform, and cross-sectionally rank/z-score within each PIT universe — never differencing or summing the raw series.
8. Apply the universe filter: US-listed common equity only (exclude ETFs, ADRs/foreign, restricted/units/warrants) passing price, ADV, market-cap, and borrowability floors, evaluated point-in-time.
9. Run two backtest engines, both keyed on pit_available_date: (a) semi-monthly-rebalanced decile long-short, size/sector-neutral; (b) calendar-time event-study portfolios on signal-crossing / threshold-list events.
10. Apply the cost model: borrow fee on the short leg, slippage∝1/ADV, commissions, and turnover cost; re-express punitive-borrow shorts via listed puts; produce net-of-cost return streams.
11. Evaluate the metric suite with regime sub-samples, multiple-testing corrections (deflated Sharpe, FDR), and walk-forward out-of-sample splits; emit reproducible diagnostics (per-rebalance breadth, drops from unmappable CUSIPs, short-leg tail stats).

## 1. How a fail happens: NSCC/CNS and the Reg SHO close-out regime

- **A fail-to-deliver (FTD) at NSCC is a NET position, not a per-trade event. NSCC novates every CNS-eligible equity trade as central counterparty and nets each member's buys and sells in a security for a given settlement date into ONE net-long or net-short position. A fail is a net-short delivery obligation the member cannot satisfy from its DTC account by settlement date.**  
  Because the fail is netted and CCP-guaranteed, it is impossible from the CNS position alone to attribute the fail to a specific trade, counterparty, or cause (naked short vs. long-in-transit vs. operational).
- **CNS is literally 'continuous': an unsatisfied delivery obligation is not cancelled but carried forward as an aged open short position and re-attempted (typically each night) as shares arrive; receiving members with open long positions are allocated on an age-based priority. NSCC guarantees the receiving side regardless.**  
  One member's aged fail leaves counterparties holding NSCC-guaranteed but unreceived long positions - the structural fact that fuels 'phantom share' narratives, even though the position is fully collateralized and guaranteed.
- **Rule 203(b)(1) 'locate': a broker-dealer may not accept/effect a short sale unless it has (i) borrowed the security, (ii) entered a bona-fide arrangement to borrow, or (iii) reasonable grounds to believe it can be borrowed and delivered by settlement. Rule 203(b)(2)(iii) exempts bona-fide market-making from the locate requirement.**  
  The locate is a pre-trade gatekeeper against naked shorting, but the 'reasonable grounds' standard plus the market-maker exception leave legitimate room for fails to arise.
- **Rule 204 close-out deadlines are anchored to the SETTLEMENT date, not trade date: short-sale fails must be closed out (purchase or borrow) by the beginning of regular trading hours on the settlement day FOLLOWING the settlement date ('T+1 after settlement'); long-sale fails and fails attributable to bona-fide market making by the THIRD consecutive settlement day following the settlement date ('T+3 after settlement').**  
  This is a fast, mechanical, mandatory buy-in. Short-sale fails get only one extra settlement day; the 'T+1/T+3' shorthand is measured off settlement date, so absolute trade-date timing depends on the settlement cycle.
- **Rule 204(a)(2) gives a 35-consecutive-calendar-day (T+35, from trade date) close-out window for fails on securities a person is 'deemed to own' and intends to deliver once restrictions are removed (e.g., convertibles/shelf/legend removal).**  
  This is the actual source of the 'T+35' figure widely (and often wrongly) cited in naked-short lore; it is a narrow accommodation for restricted/deemed-to-own long positions, not a general grace period.
- **Rule 204(b) 'penalty box': if a participant misses the close-out, it - and any broker-dealer to which it allocates the fail - may not accept or effect further short sales in that security without first BORROWING or arranging to borrow (pre-borrow) until the fail is closed out.**  
  This is the real enforcement teeth; it converts a delivery failure into a hard pre-borrow constraint that raises borrow demand and loan fees on the affected name.
- **Threshold securities list: an equity with an aggregate CNS fail position of at least 10,000 shares AND at least 0.5% of the issuer's total shares outstanding, for 5 consecutive settlement days. SROs (Nasdaq, NYSE, Cboe) publish the lists daily; a security drops off after 5 consecutive settlement days below the threshold.**  
  The threshold list is the public, rules-based marker of PERSISTENT fails - the screen most retail 'naked short' analysis keys on.
- **Rule 203(b)(3) requires mandatory close-out of a fail in a THRESHOLD security that has persisted for 13 consecutive settlement days. This was the original 2005 Reg SHO close-out.**  
  It is largely superseded by Rule 204's far tighter T+1/T+3 close-out (2009), but remains on the books; the 13-day rule alone would let large fails persist for weeks, which is why Rule 204 was layered on top.
- **The SEC eliminated the Rule 203(b)(3) 'grandfather' provision in 2007 and the options market-maker exception in 2008 (effective October 17, 2008, with a one-time 35-day phase-in for pre-existing fails). Temporary Rule 204T took effect September 18, 2008 and was made permanent as Rule 204 (adopted July 27, 2009; effective July 31, 2009), which also dropped 204T's temporary market-maker pre-borrow exception.**  
  The options market-maker exception (used to hedge via reverse conversions without delivering) was the single biggest structural source of persistent, large fails in optionable stocks; its removal, plus Rule 204, sharply cut aggregate fails.
- **The SEC FTD file is published twice monthly (first-half data at month-end; second-half around the 15th of the next month), with six fields (settlement date, CUSIP, ticker, quantity of fails, description, prior-day closing price). Figures are CUMULATIVE outstanding fails as of each day; the age of fails cannot be determined; and the SEC states plainly that fails 'can occur for a number of reasons on both long and short sales... are not necessarily the result of short selling, and are not evidence of abusive short selling or naked short selling.'**  
  The file is lagged 2-4+ weeks and cannot distinguish naked-short fails from benign long/operational/restricted fails - the core reason FTD-based 'naked short' claims are not verifiable from the data itself, and why it is a poor real-time trading input.
- **The US standard settlement cycle shortened T+3 -> T+2 (effective September 5, 2017) -> T+1 (effective May 28, 2024) under Exchange Act Rule 15c6-1. Because Rule 204's deadlines are keyed to the settlement date, the absolute trade-date-relative close-out timing tightened automatically at each step.**  
  Older secondary sources quote stale 'T+4/T+6' close-out days that assumed T+3 settlement; under T+1 settlement the same rule bites much faster in calendar terms.
- **Academic evidence contradicts the naive 'FTDs = manipulation/imminent squeeze' thesis: Fotak, Raman & Yadav (2014) find greater FTDs associated with HIGHER liquidity and pricing efficiency and no evidence FTDs caused price distortions or 2008 firm failures; the SEC's mandatory pre-borrow order period coincided with LOWER liquidity and higher pricing errors.**  
  The best peer-reviewed work treats FTDs largely as a settlement/liquidity phenomenon rather than a manipulation signal - a strong prior against fading or chasing names purely on fail counts.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Fade the FTD/threshold-list 'squeeze' narrative* — A micro/small-cap appears on an SRO threshold list and/or trends on social media as a 'naked short' target, but screens as hard-to-borrow with high short interest and no fundamental catalyst; FTD spike is not accompanied by real borrow scarcity resolving upward. → Short the underlying where borrowable, or long puts when borrow is prohibitive (the option already prices the fee) (short, Weeks to months). Peer-reviewed evidence (Fotak/Raman/Yadav 2014) finds no squeeze premium in FTDs; high-fail names are typically hard-to-borrow, high-short-interest small caps that carry the documented short-interest underperformance anomaly. The 'FTD = imminent squeeze' retail thesis is largely unsupported.
- *Mechanical Rule 204 close-out buy pressure* — A low-float, hard-to-borrow name carries large, persistent aged fails and rising borrow fees as it approaches a Rule 204 forced close-out / pre-borrow penalty; detected via the borrow desk and loan-market stress, NOT the lagged public FTD file. → Tactical long equity or long-gamma calls (long, Days). Rule 204 forces buy-ins (purchase/borrow) on a hard clock; in thin, hard-to-borrow names the forced covering plus pre-borrow constraint can transiently lift price and implied vol.
- *Options-embedded borrow fee (post-OMM-exception)* — For hard-to-borrow optionable names, the borrow cost implied by put-call parity / reversal-conversion (box) diverges from the observed stock-loan fee. → Reversals/conversions and box spreads versus the actual borrow (long-short, Days to option expiry). Once the options market-maker exception was removed (2008) and Rule 204 raised the cost of failing, the stock-loan fee is impounded into option prices (Evans et al 2009; Muravyev et al 2022; Stratmann/Welborn 2013 show removal raised borrow rates and cut optionable-stock fails). The options-implied fee becomes a tradeable proxy for and hedge of the loan fee.

**Caveats:**
- The task phrasing 'removal of the bona-fide market-maker exception' is imprecise: the bona-fide market-maker exception to the LOCATE requirement (Rule 203(b)(2)(iii)) was NOT abolished and still exists, and Rule 204 still grants bona-fide market-making fails the longer T+3 close-out. What was actually removed were the Rule 203(b)(3) close-out exceptions - the 'grandfather' provision (2007) and the OPTIONS market-maker exception (2008) - plus temporary Rule 204T's separate market-maker pre-borrow exception (dropped in final Rule 204, 2009).
- The SEC FTD file cannot separate naked-short fails from benign long/operational/deemed-to-own fails; any inference of 'naked shorting' from the file alone is unsupported by the data (the SEC says so explicitly).
- The FTD file is semimonthly and lagged 2-4+ weeks and reports cumulative outstanding fails whose age is indeterminable - it is a weak real-time trading signal because most fails are already force-closed under Rule 204 before publication.
- 'T+1/T+3' close-out timing is measured from the SETTLEMENT date, not trade date; older sources cite 'T+4/T+6' assuming T+3 settlement. With T+1 settlement since May 28, 2024 (T+2 from Sept 2017), the same rule bites faster in calendar terms.
- Empirical work (Fotak/Raman/Yadav 2014) undercuts the manipulation/squeeze framing of FTDs; the tradeable angles here are contrarian or microstructure-arbitrage, not endorsements of the naked-short-squeeze thesis.
- I did not independently confirm every SEC release number for the 2008/2009 actions, so I cite the Federal Register documents and SEC press releases rather than assert release numbers; and I did not verify all coauthors of the 2022 Journal of Finance stock-lending-premium paper (cited as Muravyev et al.).

## 2. What the evidence actually says about FTDs and returns

- **The strongest FTD-specific return-predictability result is Stratmann & Welborn (2016, J. Empirical Finance): stocks with fails-to-deliver earn NEGATIVE abnormal returns that are proportional to their FTD level, shown in both an event study and a Fama-French portfolio analysis; using proprietary borrow-cost data, short sellers of both low- and high-FTD stocks earn positive estimated profits.**  
  This is the cleanest 'high FTD -> lower future returns' finding in scope. It frames FTDs as a symptom of informed short selling (nonbinding constraints), not as a bullish 'squeeze' setup.
- **Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (2015, Financial Review) find that stocks reaching Reg SHO threshold FTD levels become significantly OVERVALUED (especially where short-sale constraints bind) and then reverse, but the mispricing is hard to arbitrage because of extreme shorting costs.**  
  Same sign on future returns as Stratmann-Welborn (subsequent underperformance) but the OPPOSITE mechanism: binding constraints causing overvaluation vs. nonbinding constraints enabling informed shorting. The disagreement is central to whether FTDs are cause or symptom.
- **Boulton & Braga-Alves (2012, J. Portfolio Management) find naked short sellers behind persistent fails are CONTRARIAN, not momentum: returns are positive just before increased naked shorting and generally remain positive for several weeks after, and these traders find it hard to earn consistent profits under Reg SHO.**  
  Directly contradicts both the 'naked shorts drive prices down' narrative AND, on horizon/profitability, the Stratmann-Welborn profitable-informed-shorting result. The tension is a sample issue (extreme persistent Reg SHO fails vs. broad cross-section of FTDs).
- **Fotak, Raman & Yadav (2014, JFE; NYSE sample 2005-2008, 1,492 stocks) find greater FTDs are associated with HIGHER liquidity and pricing efficiency (lower pricing errors, lower volatility, tighter spreads), comparable to delivered short sales; the SEC's temporary pre-borrow order LOWERED liquidity and raised pricing errors. No evidence FTDs caused the 2008 declines.**  
  The core 'symptom not cause' evidence: FTDs do not manipulate prices downward and restricting them harmed market quality. Argues against a causal price-suppression channel.
- **Evans, Geczy, Musto & Reed (2009, RFS) show that in most hard-to-borrow situations a major options market maker CHOOSES to fail-to-deliver rather than borrow, and when failing is cheaper than borrowing the link between borrow costs and option prices weakens; failing is effectively an option the market maker exercises for profit.**  
  Establishes that a large share of FTDs is mechanical, non-directional market-making behavior driven by borrow economics, not bearish conviction. Undercuts reading FTD spikes as informed bearish bets.
- **Kolasinski, Reed & Thornock (2013, Financial Management) find the 2008 short-sale restrictions (pre-borrow requirement and outright bans) were associated with MORE informed short selling / larger price impact of short volume, not less.**  
  Restricting the mechanics that generate FTDs was counterproductive, consistent with FTDs/naked shorting reflecting information rather than manipulation.
- **Diether, Lee & Werner (2009, RFS, 'Short-Sale Strategies and Return Predictability') show short-selling flow predicts negative abnormal returns and a strategy on daily short-selling activity earns significant positive returns over a short (~20-trading-day) horizon; their companion 'It's SHO Time!' (2009, JF) shows the Reg SHO pilot suspending price tests left daily returns and volatility essentially unaffected.**  
  Provides the informed-short-selling backdrop that FTD predictability rides on, and shows the uptick-rule/price-test mechanics that people blame for naked shorting had little effect on prices.
- **Culp & Heaton (2008, Regulation, Cato) argue naked short selling is economically nearly equivalent to covered short selling and find no evidence it manipulatively engineers declines or harms markets.**  
  The theoretical skeptic anchor: distinguishes 'FTD as settlement artifact' from 'FTD as manipulation,' supporting the symptom interpretation.
- **FTD data is public but coarse: the SEC publishes CNS-aggregated fails semi-monthly with a multi-week lag, and explicitly warns FTDs are NOT synonymous with naked short selling (mechanical errors, long fails, and options-MM activity all generate fails). GAO-09-483 documents that post-2008 close-out rules sharply reduced FTDs.**  
  Any FTD signal is lagged, noisy, and definitionally not 'naked shorting'; the data-generating process also changed structurally after 2008/2009, limiting external validity of the 2005-2008 studies.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *FTD-level cross-sectional long-short (fade high-FTD)* — Rank US equities by FTD intensity from the SEC semi-monthly CNS fails file (shares failed scaled by shares outstanding or by dollar volume); short the high-FTD tail, long the low-FTD tail, sector/size-neutral. → US cash equities, long-short; short leg via securities lending. (long-short (short high-FTD, long low-FTD), ~2-8 weeks (Stratmann-Welborn event study / FF portfolios), accounting for the ~14-16 day data-publication lag.). Stratmann & Welborn (2016): high-FTD stocks earn negative abnormal returns proportional to FTD level; consistent with informed short selling.
- *Reg SHO threshold-list overvaluation reversal* — Single names newly on / persisting on the Reg SHO threshold list (FTDs >=0.5% of shares outstanding for 5 consecutive settlement days) with binding short constraints (high borrow fee, low lendable supply). → Long-dated puts or bearish spreads (options often cheaper than borrowing the hard-to-borrow stock); direct short only where borrow available. (short / event, Weeks to a few months (overvaluation correction).). Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (2015): threshold-FTD stocks are overvalued and reverse, but the mispricing persists precisely because it is costly to arbitrage; options route sidesteps the borrow.
- *Fade the FTD/naked-short 'squeeze' narrative* — Social/retail claims that elevated FTDs signal imminent short squeeze or manipulation-driven suppression. Treat high FTD as a mild bearish/neutral tilt, not a long thesis. → Position sizing / avoidance rule; optionally overlay on angle 1's short screen. (avoid-long / short-bias, Weeks; risk-management stance rather than a timed trade.). Stratmann-Welborn (negative forward returns), Fotak-Raman-Yadav & Culp-Heaton (no manipulation, market-quality neutral-to-positive), and Evans et al. (much FTD is mechanical options-MM failing) jointly reject 'high FTD = bullish squeeze.'
- *Daily short-volume reversal (DLW backdrop)* — FINRA daily short-sale volume (public): short the most heavily shorted, long the least, cross-sectionally. → US cash equities, long-short. (long-short, ~1-4 weeks (~20 trading days).). Diether, Lee & Werner (2009): short-selling flow predicts negative abnormal returns over roughly a 20-day horizon; adjacent to, and correlated with, the FTD signal.

**Caveats:**
- Direction consensus with mechanism dispute: multiple papers find high-FTD stocks subsequently UNDERPERFORM, but they disagree on why. Stratmann-Welborn (2016) attribute it to informed short selling under NONBINDING constraints; Autore-Boulton-Braga-Alves (2015) attribute it to overvaluation under BINDING constraints that later reverses. Same sign, opposite economics.
- Symptom vs. cause: the weight of evidence (Fotak-Raman-Yadav 2014; Culp-Heaton 2008; Kolasinski-Reed-Thornock 2013) finds NO causal price-suppression or manipulation from fails; FTDs improve or do not harm market quality and reflect information/settlement mechanics. FTD predictability of returns should be read as a symptom of informed shorting/overvaluation, not proof that fails push prices down.
- Contradiction on horizon/profitability: Boulton & Braga-Alves (2012) find returns POSITIVE around persistent naked-short fails and that these traders struggle to profit, which tensions with Stratmann-Welborn's profitable-informed-shorting result. Likely driven by sample differences (extreme persistent Reg SHO threshold fails vs. the broad FTD cross-section).
- Borrow-cost offset: high-FTD stocks are disproportionately expensive/hard to borrow, so the short-leg alpha is substantially eaten by loan fees. Stratmann-Welborn's profitability uses proprietary borrow-cost data; net-of-cost, retail-accessible profitability is far from established.
- Data limitations: SEC FTD data is CNS-aggregated, semi-monthly, and lagged multiple weeks; FTDs are explicitly NOT synonymous with naked short selling (mechanical errors, long fails, options-MM fails). Signals are noisy and not real-time.
- External validity: nearly all cited results use 2005-2008 data, before Rule 204 (2009) tightened close-outs, before the options-market-maker exception was eliminated (Sept 2008), and before settlement shortened to T+2 (2017) then T+1 (May 2024). Aggregate FTD levels fell sharply after 2008 (GAO-09-483); applicability to the 2020s regime is uncertain.
- Magnitudes not fully verified: I confirmed the SIGN (negative) and proportionality of the Stratmann-Welborn FTD return effect and the direction/horizon of the Diether-Lee-Werner predictability, but could not verify a specific basis-point-per-week magnitude for either from primary text in this session; treat quoted sizes elsewhere with caution.
- Small samples for extreme-fail studies (threshold list) raise power and survivorship concerns; the reversal/overvaluation trades live in an illiquid, hard-to-borrow, high-idiosyncratic-risk corner.

## 3. ETF fails, operational shorting, and where the real dislocations are

- **ETFs are ~10% of US equity market cap but account for >20% of short interest and ~78% of fails-to-deliver in the SEC FTD file (Evans, Moussawi, Pagano & Sedunov).**  
  The FTD file is dominated by ETFs largely for structural, not manipulative, reasons; naive FTD/short-squeeze screens that don't strip ETFs are mostly reading operational noise.
- **'Operational shorting' is when an ETF market maker/AP sells shares in the secondary market that have not yet been created, then cures the resulting fail by creating shares via the primary market. It constitutes a MAJORITY of ETF short interest.**  
  Most ETF short interest and fails are a liquidity-provision by-product with a self-curing supply mechanism (create-to-lend), not directional/informed bets — so high ETF SI is not a squeeze setup the way it is for a single stock.
- **Reg SHO Rule 204 (Release 34-60388, adopted 27 Jul 2009) gives a bona-fide-market-making close-out at the third consecutive settlement day after settlement date — 'T+6' under the then-prevailing T+3 cycle — and a 35-calendar-day (T+35) window for 'deemed-to-own' securities.**  
  These extended close-out windows are exactly what let APs/MMs deliver late and cure fails by creating shares; they are the regulatory engine behind operational shorting. Note: T+2 (2017) and T+1 (May 2024) settlement compressed the absolute calendar window, but the mechanic is unchanged.
- **Evans et al. find operational shorting is associated with improved ETF liquidity and greater price efficiency in underlying securities, plus higher retail trading and short-term ETF return reversals — but also with increased systemic risk.**  
  Confirms the benign/liquidity interpretation for the typical case while flagging the tail: the same mechanism can amplify stress, which is where the tradeable dislocations live.
- **Pan & Zeng ('ETF Arbitrage Under Liquidity Mismatch'): when a liquid ETF holds illiquid assets (corporate bonds), the AP's dual role as bond dealer and ETF arbitrageur creates an inventory-management conflict that can distort or halt arbitrage, producing large, persistent ETF-vs-NAV mispricings.**  
  Explains why the tradeable dislocations concentrate in illiquid-underlying ETFs (corporate/HY bonds, munis, EM, bank loans), not in tight-arb equity ETFs like SPY.
- **March 2020 'dash for cash': bond ETFs traded at large discounts to NAV (AGG ~-4.4%, BND ~-6.2%, LQD ~-4.5% on 16 Mar 2020) as AP arbitrage broke down — a real-world instance of Pan-Zeng liquidity mismatch, relieved only after the Fed's SMCCF backstop.**  
  The canonical tradeable dislocation: premium/discount blowouts in illiquid-underlying ETFs during funding stress, with a partly mean-reverting component.
- **XRT (SPDR S&P Retail) is persistently the most-shorted US ETF, with short interest that has repeatedly exceeded 100% of shares outstanding (etf.com reported figures in the hundreds of percent of assets) and frequent Reg SHO threshold-list appearances; create-to-lend keeps borrow available.**  
  Sector ETFs like XRT function as a naked-short channel for expressing views on hard-to-borrow constituents; because APs can create shares to lend, elastic supply mutes classic squeeze dynamics even at SI>100%.
- **Shim & Todorov ('ETFs, Illiquid Assets, and Fire Sales', BIS WP 975) show the AP holds bond inventory and acts as a buffer between the ETF and the underlying, behaving asymmetrically on creation vs redemption, which lets ETFs avoid the fire-sale dynamics of open-end mutual funds.**  
  Explains why ETF fails/redemptions are usually absorbed benignly (AP warehousing) and why the ETF wrapper is more fire-sale-resistant than a mutual fund — bounding the systemic-risk read.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Strip ETFs from single-stock FTD/squeeze screens* — Any signal built on the SEC semimonthly FTD file or on raw short-interest ranks. → Single-name US equities (signal-construction filter, not a position itself). (event, n/a (data hygiene)). ~78% of FTDs are ETF operational fails with no directional content (Evans et al.); leaving ETFs in the file contaminates single-stock FTD/squeeze factors. Excluding ETF CUSIPs (or modeling them separately) raises precision on the single-name fails that actually carry information.
- *Do NOT trade ETF 'short squeezes' on high SI* — ETF short interest > 100% of shares outstanding (e.g., XRT), threshold-list appearance, rising borrow fee. → XRT and similarly hard-to-borrow sector ETFs. (event, n/a (anti-signal)). Unlike single stocks, ETF share supply is elastic via create-to-lend, so SI>100% reflects operational demand, not a cornered float. Classic squeeze setups fail here; the durable edge is on the lending side (collect elevated fees), not on betting for a forced buy-in.
- *Illiquid-underlying ETF NAV-discount convergence* — Persistent, wide discount of ETF price to NAV/iNAV in corporate-bond, HY, muni, EM, or bank-loan ETFs during funding stress (e.g., LQD/HYG/AGG at 4-6% discounts in Mar 2020). → LQD, HYG, AGG, muni and EM-debt ETFs; pairs vs NAV proxies or vs futures. (long, days to weeks). Pan-Zeng liquidity mismatch: AP arbitrage breaks down under bond illiquidity, opening discounts that historically partly revert as arbitrage/backstops restore. A fraction of the 'discount' is genuine price discovery on stale NAV, so only the excess over fair repricing is the edge.
- *Harvest create-to-lend borrow fees on hard-to-borrow ETFs* — Sustained high cost-to-borrow with active create-to-lend (elastic supply) on names like XRT. → Long the ETF, lend it out via a securities-lending program. (long, weeks to months). Persistent operational short demand means lenders of hard-to-borrow ETFs earn elevated fees with low squeeze risk (supply is created to meet demand). Income-like carry rather than a directional bet on the sector.

**Caveats:**
- ETF fails are usually structurally benign: operational shorting self-cures via creation, so an FTD or SI spike in a liquid equity ETF (SPY, IWM) is almost never a tradeable dislocation. The tradeable cases are concentrated in ILLIQUID-underlying ETFs under stress.
- 'T+6' is a legacy figure from the T+3 settlement era. US settlement moved to T+2 (Sept 2017) and T+1 (May 2024), compressing the absolute close-out calendar even though the Rule 204 mechanics (bona-fide-MM close-out at the third settlement day after settlement date; T+35 deemed-to-own) are unchanged. Verify current day-counts before relying on a specific number.
- Pan & Zeng, 'ETF Arbitrage Under Liquidity Mismatch', is a working paper (ESRB WP 59, 2017; R&R at the Journal of Finance as of 2023 per the author's site) — I could not confirm final journal placement, so treat it as a working paper, not a published article.
- Evans, Moussawi, Pagano & Sedunov circulated for years as 'ETF Short Interest and Failures-to-Deliver: Naked Short-Selling or Operational Shorting?' and is forthcoming/published in the Journal of Financial Economics (a 2026 ScienceDirect DOI exists); the exact final volume/pages were not confirmable via the paywalled page.
- Shim & Todorov's create-to-lend/inventory work is cited here from BIS Working Paper 975 (2021); I could not verify its final peer-reviewed journal placement. The specific 'create-to-lend' terminology is most directly developed in Evans et al. and in Todorov's bond-ETF arbitrage work, not necessarily coined by Shim & Todorov.
- NAV-discount 'convergence' is dangerous: end-of-day NAV can be stale/model-priced, so an observed discount partly reflects true underlying repricing that has not hit NAV yet. In March 2020 the gap only closed after the Fed's SMCCF announcement — i.e., convergence can require a policy backstop, not just patience.
- The XRT '526% of assets' short-interest figure comes from etf.com reporting and varies over time; use current Ortex/Fintel/S3 data for any live position. SI-as-%-of-shares-outstanding and SI-as-%-of-float differ, and ETF creation changes the denominator.
- This is predominantly a mechanics/skeptic topic: the highest-conviction 'edge' is defensive (avoiding false FTD/squeeze signals) rather than a standalone alpha source. The directional angles carry real basis, liquidity, and backstop-timing risk.

## 4. Options-driven fails and "reset" transactions

- **The governing regime is Reg SHO: Rule 200(g) requires accurate long/short order marking; Rule 203(b)(1) requires a locate before a short sale; Rule 203(b)(2)(iii) gives registered market makers a locate exemption for bona fide market making; Rule 204 requires a clearing participant to close out a short-sale fail-to-deliver (FTD) by borrowing or PURCHASING like-kind-and-quantity shares by the start of regular trading hours on the settlement day after settlement date, with a longer window for long-sale and bona-fide-MM fails.**  
  Every reset scheme is an attempt to fake the 'purchase' leg of Rule 204 (or the locate under 203(b)) without ever taking real long delivery. Knowing which rule each leg targets tells you which data field (order marks, locate logs, CNS FTD aging, buy-in notices) will show the anomaly.
- **A 'reset transaction' pairs a stock purchase that appears to close out an FTD with an option leg engineered to strip the just-bought stock right back out. Classic form: a deep-in-the-money BUY-WRITE (buy stock, write a deep-ITM call almost certain to be exercised/assigned next day) so the long is removed and the short/FTD is re-created; married-put and reverse-conversion variants do the same with puts. Options are often 1-day FLEX or very short-dated listed contracts.**  
  Net economic position is essentially unchanged, but the books show a 'purchase,' resetting the Rule 204 close-out clock indefinitely. The tell is that the closing purchase is systematically reversed within ~1 day by option exercise/assignment rather than held.
- **SEC OCIE issued a Risk Alert on Aug 9, 2013, 'Strengthening Practices for Preventing and Detecting Illegal Options Trading Used to Reset Reg SHO Close-out Obligations' (see also SEC Press Release 2013-151), explicitly flagging deep-ITM buy-writes/married puts, short-dated FLEX and very-short-dated listed options as tools to fake close-outs. It stresses that such sham close-outs violate Reg SHO.**  
  This is the primary regulator playbook naming the exact instruments and red flags; it is the canonical detection checklist and the document that made the phrase 'reset transactions' a term of art.
- **SEC's first naked-short-selling enforcement actions (Aug 5, 2009, PR 2009-179) charged Hazan Capital Management/Steven M. Hazan and TJM Proprietary Trading (Benson/Burke) for using REVERSE CONVERSIONS in Reg SHO threshold securities to fake compliance and improperly claim the market-maker locate exception; settlements ~ $3.0M (Hazan) and ~$541K (TJM).**  
  Establishes that reverse conversions with a paired counterparty in threshold names were the original enforced fact pattern, and that abusing the bona-fide-MM locate exemption is a distinct, chargeable leg.
- **optionsXpress: SEC charged the firm, CFO Thomas Stern, and customer Jonathan Feldman (PR 2012-66, Apr 16, 2012) for a multi-year series of sham resets (Oct 2008-Mar 2010); Feldman alone bought ~$2.9B of stock and sold short ~$1.7B of options in 2009. ALJ initial decision June 7, 2013; SEC Commission Opinion Aug 18, 2016 (Sec. Act Rel. 10125 / Exch. Act Rel. 78621, AP 3-14848) affirmed Rule 204/204T violations and imposed disgorgement plus a third-tier penalty, but found the Division had NOT proven fraud against Feldman.**  
  Shows the strategy scales to billions, that liability attaches at the clearing broker for the close-out failure even where customer fraud is not proven, and that a purely mechanical Rule 204 violation is independently actionable.
- **Exchanges reinforced this via regulatory circulars — e.g., CBOE Regulatory Circular RG08-063 (May 19, 2008), 'Regulation SHO (Short Sales) Close-Out Requirement, Use of Option Transactions,' and later RG12-022 on Rule 204 close-outs — warning members that option transactions cannot be used to satisfy close-out obligations. FINRA's Exam/Risk Monitoring reports (2023-2025) repeatedly cite improper bona-fide-MM claims and improper reuse of locates for hard-to-borrow/threshold names.**  
  Confirms the enforcement is cross-SRO and ongoing, and that 'reuse of locates' and 'non-bona-fide market making' are the live exam priorities a detection model should mirror.
- **The economic engine is that in hard-to-borrow (HTB) names, the option-implied 'synthetic' (built from puts/calls, i.e., a reverse conversion) trades cheaper than actual shares because failing to deliver is often cheaper than borrowing. Evans, Geczy, Musto & Reed (2009) show a large options market maker chooses to FAIL rather than borrow in most HTB cases, and that value passes into option prices.**  
  The synthetic-vs-real discount is both the profit motive for resets AND a measurable signal: an unusually negative implied borrow rate from option parity flags names where FTD/borrow stress and reset activity are most likely.
- **Dividend-arbitrage FTD spikes: around ex-dividend dates, deep-ITM calls should be exercised for the dividend, but Hao, Kalay & Mayhew (2010) document that a large fraction of holders irrationally fail to exercise, and sophisticated traders run coordinated deep-ITM option 'dividend plays' to harvest that windfall. In HTB names these dividend-capture reversals/short legs produce transient FTD spikes clustered on the day before/at ex-date.**  
  Creates a predictable calendar pattern in FTD and option-volume data (and in short selling around ex-div) that both mimics and masks reset activity, so ex-dividend timing must be controlled for when reading FTD data.
- **FTD and threshold data are public: SEC publishes semi-monthly Fails-to-Deliver data (sec.gov/data-research), and SROs publish daily threshold-security lists (a name qualifies with aggregate CNS fails >=10,000 shares AND >=0.5% of shares outstanding persisting 5 consecutive settlement days). Settlement moved to T+1 on May 28, 2024, compressing all Rule 204 close-out windows.**  
  These are the ground-truth inputs for any detection or trade signal. T+1 shortened the clock reset schemes must beat, and made persistent multi-week FTDs a stronger anomaly than under T+3.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *FTD-persistence + borrow-fee squeeze screen* — Names on SRO threshold lists / SEC semi-monthly FTD data showing large, MULTI-WEEK persistent fails that survive the compressed T+1 Rule 204 window, coincident with rising and elevated stock-borrow fees. → Equity (or long calls / risk-reversals) on the flagged single names; borrow fee from a securities-finance vendor. (long / long-volatility (or simply avoid initiating shorts), Days to a few weeks). Persistent fails plus rich borrow indicate real deliverable scarcity and forced-buy-in / squeeze potential; the crowd that is short-and-failing is a fragile fuel supply. This reads reset/FTD stress as a squeeze-risk gauge, not as endorsement of the illegal conduct.
- *Option-implied negative borrow (parity dislocation)* — Put-call parity / conversion box on a HTB name implies a large negative rebate (synthetic short much cheaper than shares), diverging from the quoted borrow fee. → Listed options (measure via box/conversion); trade only if you have genuine access to borrow to run a real conversion, otherwise use as a risk flag. (long-short / arbitrage-conditional, Intraday to days). Evans-Geczy-Musto-Reed show fail-value bleeds into option prices; the implied-borrow gap identifies the exact HTB names where FTD stress, squeeze risk, and (illicitly) reset activity concentrate. As a clean arb it is largely institutional because it needs locate/borrow and low fees.
- *Ex-dividend FTD/short-vol calendar filter* — Around ex-dividend dates in HTB dividend payers, deep-ITM call open interest is large and FTD/short-selling data spike, then revert within days. → None as standalone alpha; used to de-noise the FTD screens above and to AVOID writing deep-ITM calls into the dividend crowd (assignment risk). (event (risk-avoidance / filter), 1-5 days around ex-date). Hao-Kalay-Mayhew show the 'dividend play' is a low-cost, market-maker-dominated trading game; retail writers mostly get assigned. Treating the ex-date FTD spike as noise (not a persistent-fail signal) avoids false squeeze reads and avoids being on the wrong side of the exercise game.

**Caveats:**
- Executing sham reset transactions, faking locates, or falsely claiming bona-fide market-making status to evade Reg SHO close-out/locate rules is illegal (Reg SHO Rules 203/204 and, where deception is proven, Section 10(b)/17(a)); this brief is detection and market-structure knowledge only, not a how-to.
- Correlation, not proof: persistent FTDs, threshold-list membership, deep-ITM buy-write clusters, and negative implied borrow are consistent with legal activity (real dividend arb, hedging, genuine market making, T+2 operational fails) as well as with abuse. None is dispositive; regulators corroborate with account-level order marks, locate logs, counterparty concentration, and intent evidence.
- SEC/SRO FTD data is aggregate, net at CNS, semi-monthly and lagged; it cannot be attributed to a specific account or strategy and understates gross fails. Treat single-name FTD spikes as hypotheses, not confirmed manipulation.
- Rule 204 day-counts shifted with the move to T+1 settlement on May 28, 2024 (short-sale fails now close out ~one settlement day after T+1 settlement; longer for long-sale/MM fails). Older sources citing 'T+3/T+4/T+6' describe the pre-2024 clock — verify the current window before relying on exact days.
- In optionsXpress the Commission sustained the Rule 204/204T close-out violations but did NOT find fraud proven against the customer (Feldman); do not overstate the cases as across-the-board fraud findings. Penalty/appeal specifics beyond the 2016 Commission Opinion were not independently verified here.
- I could not machine-read the SEC 2013 Risk Alert PDF or the CBOE circular PDFs directly (they rendered as binary); the red-flag list and CBOE dates/titles were verified via SEC press materials, search snippets, and secondary legal summaries rather than the primary PDFs word-for-word. The exact enumerated red-flag wording should be confirmed against the source PDF.
- The tradeable angles are indirect, capacity-constrained, and data-intensive; the genuine parity/borrow arbitrage is largely institutional (needs cheap borrow and low fees), and the ex-dividend option 'game' is dominated by low-cost market makers. Retail edge here is mostly risk-avoidance, not alpha.

## 5. Getting the data, and the pitfalls baked into it

- **The SEC publishes FTD data free at sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/fails-deliver-data as pipe-delimited ('|') ASCII text files. Record layout: SETTLEMENT DATE (8-digit YYYYMMDD numeric); CUSIP (9 chars); SYMBOL (ticker, up to 10 chars); QUANTITY (FAILS) = total fail-to-deliver shares outstanding (numeric); DESCRIPTION (company name, up to 30 chars); PRICE (closing price; the layout labels it the prior day's close).**  
  You must parse fixed-schema pipe-delimited files and key records on CUSIP, not on the symbol/description strings, which are as-of-then and can be stale or blank.
- **Cadence and lag: files post twice a month — the first half of a month at ~end of that month, the second half at ~the 15th of the following month. The SEC explicitly warns it 'cannot guarantee that the data will be posted by a particular date.' Any given FTD is roughly 2-6 weeks stale by publication.**  
  FTD data is structurally lagged. It is unusable as a fast/event signal; it can only support slow cross-sectional analysis, and backtests must stamp each row with its true public-availability date to avoid look-ahead of several weeks.
- **The QUANTITY field is a SNAPSHOT of the cumulative balance of OPEN fails-to-deliver as of that settlement date — prior open fails plus new fails minus fails that settled — NOT the count of new fails that day. The SEC states the figure 'may have little or no relationship to yesterday's aggregate' and that the age and source of fails cannot be determined.**  
  You cannot difference two consecutive settlement dates to infer 'new fails,' and you cannot sum a time series as if each day were a fresh flow. Treating the snapshot as a flow is the single most common analytical error.
- **Data are aggregated across ALL NSCC members from the National Securities Clearing Corporation's Continuous Net Settlement (CNS) system, equities only. Participant/obligation-level (firm-attributed) CNS data is confidential and NOT public; the SEC releases only the aggregate balance per security.**  
  You cannot attribute fails to any specific broker, market maker, or short seller, and you cannot see who is failing or why — precluding claims of naked shorting by a named party.
- **Coverage runs from February 2004 to present, but with a reporting-threshold discontinuity: records before September 16, 2008 include only securities with a balance of at least 10,000 fail shares; on/after September 16, 2008 the file includes ALL securities with any open-fail balance.**  
  The pre-2008 era has a 10,000-share floor (small fails are censored), so pre- and post-2008 series are not directly comparable and pooling them introduces threshold/survivorship bias.
- **The SEC attaches explicit interpretive caveats: 'Fails-to-deliver are not necessarily the result of short selling, and are not evidence of abusive short selling or naked short selling'; and for PRICE, when unavailable or under a penny the field is a '.', and the SEC 'cannot guarantee that this price matches closing prices available from other sources.'**  
  Fails include long-sale fails, operational/processing fails, and bona-fide market-maker fails — not just shorts. The bundled price field is unreliable; recompute dollar-fails from a clean price source rather than trusting the file's PRICE.
- **FTDs are governed by Regulation SHO. Rule 204 requires participants to close out fail positions (buy-in/borrow) shortly after the settlement date; Rule 203(b)(3) forces close-out of 'threshold securities' with fails persisting 13 consecutive settlement days; a bona-fide market-maker exception lets options market makers short without a locate. The T+1 settlement transition (effective May 28, 2024) compressed Rule 204 close-out timelines.**  
  The close-out regime bounds how long large fails persist and makes threshold-list membership economically meaningful; the 2008 (Rule 204) and 2024 (T+1) regime changes mean FTD dynamics are not stationary across eras.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Cross-sectional high-FTD short (constraint/overvaluation basket)* — Rank stocks by open FTD normalized to shares outstanding (or dollar-fails = FTD x price / ADV), point-in-time as of each file's true publication date; go short the top decile of persistently high-FTD names, ideally confirmed by elevated borrow fees. → Cash equities (short) or listed put options on the constrained names; basket rebalanced semi-monthly on publication. (short, 1-3 months (matched to the semi-monthly cadence and multi-week publication lag).). Stratmann & Welborn (2016) find FTD stocks earn negative abnormal returns proportional to FTD level, and Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (2015) link fails to short-sale constraints and overvaluation. FTD proxies binding constraints/informed shorting. Note the countervailing evidence (Fotak, Raman & Yadav, 2014) that FTDs are largely benign, so treat effect size as modest and possibly decayed post-Rule-204.
- *Reg SHO threshold-list close-out event* — Flag securities entering/persisting on exchange threshold-security lists (fails >=10,000 shares and >=0.5% of shares outstanding for 13 consecutive settlement days), which triggers mandatory Rule 204/203 buy-ins. → Equities or short-dated calls on the affected name. (event, Days to two weeks around the forced close-out window.). Forced buy-ins in an already hard-to-borrow, constrained name can produce short, sharp upward pressure. Highly speculative and crowded; borrow is already expensive and the SEC FTD file's lag means the threshold list (published by exchanges/FINRA, more timely) is the actionable input, not the semi-monthly FTD file itself.
- *FTD-plus-borrow confirmation overlay* — Use FTD level only as a corroborating flag on top of a short-sale-constraint anomaly (high borrow fee, high utilization, high short interest); require both FTD presence and expensive borrow before shorting. → Long-short equity: short constrained/high-FTD names, long unconstrained matched peers. (long-short, ~1 month rebalance.). FTD alone is noisy (long fails, operational fails, MM fails); intersecting it with borrow-cost data isolates genuinely constrained, likely-overvalued names and hedges factor exposure. FTD adds marginal information about constraint bindingness beyond borrow fee.

**Caveats:**
- Snapshot, not flow: the QUANTITY field is a cumulative open-fail balance, so you must never difference consecutive settlement dates to get 'new fails' nor sum the series over time. This is the SEC's own explicit warning and the dominant misuse of the dataset.
- Structural lag of ~2-6 weeks (first half at month-end, second half ~the 15th of the next month) makes the data useless for fast/event trading and mandates point-in-time stamping (stamp each row with actual availability date) to avoid multi-week look-ahead bias in any backtest.
- Aggregated across all NSCC members; participant/obligation-level CNS data is confidential and not public, so fails cannot be attributed to a named firm, short seller, or to 'naked shorting.' FTDs are also not necessarily short sales — they include long-sale fails, operational/processing fails, and bona-fide market-maker fails.
- CUSIP-keyed: needs a point-in-time CUSIP-to-ticker crosswalk (CUSIPs get reassigned, tickers recycled) and corporate-action handling. Raw share counts are not comparable across splits; the file's own SYMBOL/DESCRIPTION/PRICE are as-of-then and the SEC warns the price may not match other sources and is '.' when missing or sub-penny.
- Regime discontinuities break stationarity: the 10,000-share reporting floor before Sept 16, 2008 censors small fails (don't pool pre/post-2008); Rule 204 (2008-09) and the T+1 settlement transition (effective May 28, 2024) both changed close-out mechanics and likely the FTD distribution.
- The academic evidence is genuinely mixed and not a consensus: Fotak, Raman & Yadav (2014) find FTDs are largely benign and even liquidity/efficiency-enhancing with no evidence they caused 2008 price distortions, while Stratmann & Welborn (2016) and Autore et al. (2015) find negative-return predictability. Any tradeable claim should be treated as modest, contested, and possibly decayed under the post-2008/2024 close-out regimes.
- I could not independently verify the exact adopting-release number for the T+1 settlement rule in this session; the effective date (May 28, 2024) and its compression of Rule 204 close-out timelines are verified via SEC and law-firm sources, but treat the release-number detail as unconfirmed.
- Third-party vendors (Fintel, ChartExchange, stockgrid.io, ForcedAlpha, and others) largely re-serve the same free SEC file with their own CUSIP-to-ticker mapping and charting; their added value is convenience and mapping, not new data, and any mapping/corporate-action errors they make propagate silently. NSCC/CNS-level data behind the aggregate remains unavailable from any of them.

## 6. Documented & practitioner strategies (the record behind the catalog)

- **SEC publishes FTD data twice a month (FOIA files, per-CUSIP, back to 2004). Each number is the NSCC/CNS aggregate NET fail balance outstanding on a given settlement date, released with a lag. It is a stock (level), not a flow, and conflates short-sale fails, long-sale/operational fails, ETF creation/redemption fails, and Rule 144 delivery delays; it carries no counterparty and does not isolate 'naked' shorting.**  
  Every FTD signal inherits these limitations: netting hides gross activity, the level/flow confusion breaks naive momentum reads, the lag defeats fast timing, and 'high FTD = naked short/manipulation' is an unproven inference. Signals must be built on the level's persistence and its ratio to float/volume, not on the raw number.
- **Reg SHO threshold-security definition (17 CFR 242.203(c)(6)): a stock is added to an exchange's threshold list after aggregate CNS fails are >=10,000 shares AND >=0.5% of shares outstanding for 5 consecutive settlement days, and removed after 5 consecutive days below that level. Lists are public daily (NYSE, Nasdaq, Cboe).**  
  This gives a clean, dated, public event (add/remove) usable for event studies and screens -- the only crisp, unambiguous FTD-derived trigger. It is a coarse filter (0.5% of float is a low bar for microcaps) and lags the underlying fails by days.
- **Close-out timing is asymmetric and mostly fast. Under Rule 204, a short-sale fail must be closed out by the open of the settlement day after settlement date (T+4 in the T+3 era; ~T+2 now under T+1 settlement, effective May 28, 2024); a bona fide market-maker fail gets a few extra days. Only the narrow Rule 204(a)(2) category -- fails on a sale of shares the seller is 'deemed to own' but cannot yet deliver (Rule 144 restricted stock, pending conversions/transfers) -- gets the T+35 CALENDAR-day window. Failure to close out puts the broker in the 'penalty box' (pre-borrow required for further shorts).**  
  This is the crux for the retail 'T+35 forced buy-in' thesis: the 35-day clock is a special-case rule for deemed-to-own delayed deliveries, NOT the default clock for naked/short fails, which close out in days. The popular claim that ordinary short FTDs are systematically parked for exactly 35 days and then trigger a dated buy-in misstates the rule.
- **Academic evidence points, on average, the SAME direction: high-FTD stocks subsequently UNDERPERFORM. Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (2015) find Reg SHO threshold stocks become significantly overvalued and then reverse, with the worst overpricing where short-sale constraints bind; Stratmann & Welborn (2016) find negative abnormal returns proportional to FTD levels, consistent with informed short selling. Sample periods are 2005-2008.**  
  The dominant, replicated finding is bearish/contrarian-to-the-spike (a short-or-avoid signal), not bullish. The popular 'high FTD => imminent squeeze up' read is the opposite of the average academic result; squeezes are rare tail outcomes, not the base rate.
- **Counterintuitively, Autore et al. (2015) find threshold stocks with LOW short interest become more overvalued than threshold stocks with high short interest, and the mispricing is hard to arbitrage because these are precisely the extreme-cost-to-borrow names.**  
  It warns against the simple 'FTD + high short interest = strong short signal' composite; the overvaluation is worst where borrow is most constrained, i.e., where the short is least implementable. Edge and implementability are inversely related here.
- **FTDs are dominated by ETFs, not single names. Evans, Moussawi, Pagano & Sedunov document ETFs as ~10% of equity market cap but >20% of short interest and ~78% of failures-to-deliver, driven by 'operational shorting' -- authorized participants selling not-yet-created ETF shares under a market-making exemption. This improves liquidity/pricing efficiency but clusters by AP and proxies counterparty/systemic risk.**  
  Most FTD volume is benign plumbing, not stealth manipulation. For single-stock signals you must strip ETF operational fails. The AP-clustering result reframes ETF FTDs as a counterparty/tail-risk monitor rather than a directional alpha source.
- **Foundational work (Boni 2006) showed pre-Reg-SHO that ~42% of listed and ~47% of unlisted stocks had persistent 5+ day fails, concentrated where borrow was expensive -- i.e., market makers 'strategically fail' when borrowing is costly. Fotak, Raman & Yadav (2014, 1,492 NYSE stocks, 2005-2008) find greater FTDs associate with HIGHER liquidity and pricing efficiency and find no evidence FTDs distorted prices or caused financial-firm failures in 2008.**  
  This is the skeptic's anchor: much FTD is a rational settlement/borrow-cost artifact, and the peer-reviewed causal-harm case for FTDs is weak. It caps how much predictive 'manipulation alpha' one should expect from raw FTD spikes.
- **The only peer-reviewed 'T+35 cycle' support is Pastorek, Drabek & Albrecht (2023, Czech Journal of Economics and Finance) using wavelet coherence on GameStop-related ETF FTDs; they report consistent ~T+35 cycles and weaker T+3-to-T+6 cycles. Vendor 'squeeze scores' (e.g., ORTEX Type 1/2/3, composites of short interest, utilization, cost-to-borrow, days-to-cover, momentum) publish only self-reported, in-sample, gross-of-cost backtests.**  
  The strongest cycle evidence is a single-asset, meme-era, spectral-coherence study -- cyclicality is not a demonstrated tradeable price edge, and one asset over one episode is highly exposed to data-mining. No FTD/squeeze vendor score has credible independent, net-of-cost, out-of-sample validation.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Threshold-list / extreme-FTD overvaluation fade* — Single stock enters an exchange Reg SHO threshold list (or CNS fails spike to an extreme multiple of a stable float baseline) WITH rich valuation and, per Autore et al., relatively low reported short interest; screen out ETFs and Rule 144/convertible situations. → Long puts / put spreads or bearish risk-reversals (borrow is usually unavailable or punitive, so express short via options rather than a cash short). (short, Weeks to ~3 months (overpricing peaks near listing and reverses thereafter).). Replicated academic result: threshold/high-FTD stocks are overvalued and subsequently earn negative abnormal returns (Autore-Boulton-Braga-Alves 2015; Stratmann-Welborn 2016).
- *Hard-to-borrow forced-squeeze long (tail lottery)* — FTD level elevated AND utilization near 100% AND cost-to-borrow spiking AND days-to-cover high AND price already turning up on rising volume -- the classic multi-factor squeeze stack. → Long shares or long calls / call spreads on the single name; size as a lottery ticket. (long, Days to a couple of weeks.). Mechanically plausible (forced buy-ins + no borrow can feed a squeeze), but the academic base rate for high-FTD names is negative, and the only 'edge' evidence is unvalidated vendor backtests -- so this is a low-hit-rate, convex tail trade, not a positive-expectancy core signal.
- *Synthetic-short vs. borrow relative value on extreme-HTB names* — On names where failing-to-deliver is cheaper than borrowing (very high borrow fee), Evans et al. show part of that value leaks into option prices -- puts cheapen relative to calls, distorting put-call parity / the conversion-reversal basis. → Options reversals/conversions and box-style relative-value structures; or simply choosing the cheaper of synthetic-short-via-options vs. cash short. (long-short, Position-level / execution horizon (days to weeks).). Documented that borrow-cost-to-options-price linkage weakens when MMs fail instead of borrow (Evans, Geczy, Musto, Reed 2009) -- an execution/relative-value edge, not a naked directional call.
- *ETF FTD counterparty/tail-risk monitor (not directional alpha)* — Sustained rise in ETF operational-shorting FTDs clustered by authorized participant / high-leverage APs. → Risk overlay -- widen hedges, reduce gross, or buy tail protection on affected ETFs/sectors; not a standalone entry. (event, Regime/risk-management horizon (weeks to months).). Evans-Moussawi-Pagano-Sedunov link operational-shorting FTDs to a build-up in counterparty/systemic risk that commonalizes across ETFs sharing an AP -- useful as a risk gauge, with no established return-predictive edge.
- *T+35 'forced buy-in' calendar timing (SKEPTIC -- do not trade as stated)* — Retail thesis: count 35 (calendar or trading) days from an FTD spike and expect a dated buy-in / price pop. → n/a (angle fails scrutiny). (event, n/a). The T+35 clock applies only to Rule 204(a)(2) 'deemed to own' delayed deliveries (Rule 144, conversions), not ordinary short fails, which close out in days. The one peer-reviewed support (Pastorek et al. 2023) finds spectral cyclicality in a SINGLE meme-era asset via wavelet coherence -- periodicity, not a net-of-cost tradeable price edge. Treat as unvalidated; not a reliable timing rule.

**Caveats:**
- The core FTD anomalies (threshold overvaluation, FTD-proportional negative returns) were documented on 2004-2008 data. McLean & Pontiff (2016) show published anomalies decay ~58% post-publication; combined with the 2009 Rule 204 close-out/penalty-box regime and the May 2024 move to T+1 settlement -- both of which sharply cut FTD persistence -- the live edge today is very likely smaller than the papers imply and unverified out-of-sample.
- The dominant academic result is BEARISH for high-FTD single names (overvaluation that reverts down), which is the opposite of the popular retail 'high FTD => squeeze up' framing. Squeezes are rare tail events, not the base rate.
- The overvaluation edge concentrates in exactly the hardest-to-borrow, highest-fee, lowest-liquidity names -- so the theoretical short alpha is offset by large carry/borrow costs and slippage, and may not survive net of costs (the papers themselves flag it as difficult to arbitrage).
- SEC FTD data is a lagged, semi-monthly, netted level that conflates short fails, long fails, ETF operational shorting, and Rule 144 delays with no counterparty -- so 'FTD spike = naked shorting/manipulation' is an unproven inference, and ~78% of all FTDs are ETF plumbing that must be excluded for single-stock work.
- The T+35 thesis rests on a rule mis-specification (T+35 is a narrow deemed-to-own provision, not the clock for naked shorts) plus a single-asset, meme-era spectral study; I found no credible evidence that a mechanical T+35 calendar rule produces tradeable, net-of-cost price predictability.
- Vendor 'squeeze scores' (ORTEX, and composites of short interest/utilization/borrow fee/days-to-cover/momentum) publish only self-reported, in-sample, gross-of-cost backtests with no stop-losses; I could not verify any independent, out-of-sample validation, so their headline return figures should be treated as marketing, not evidence.
- I could not obtain exact numeric overvaluation magnitudes or a precise reversal half-life from Autore et al. (paywalled); those are stated qualitatively (significant overpricing, subsequent reversal over weeks-to-months) rather than as verified point estimates.

## 7. Historical episodes

- **A 'threshold security' under Reg SHO Rule 203(c)(6) is one with aggregate FTDs at a registered clearing agency (NSCC) of 10,000+ shares AND at least 0.5% of shares outstanding, for five consecutive settlement days; SROs (NYSE, Nasdaq, Cboe) publish the list daily.**  
  This is the only public, near-real-time, per-name FTD signal. It defines the objective bar every episode below is measured against, and it is a screenable daily dataset.
- **SEC FTD data itself is published biweekly with a ~2-4 week lag (first-half of month at month-end; second-half around the 15th of next month), per settlement date, per CUSIP, with total fail share counts — and the SEC explicitly states FTDs 'are not necessarily the result of short selling, and are not evidence of abusive or naked short selling.'**  
  The lag and the official 'FTDs are benign' framing bound what is tradeable: the raw FTD series is stale by the time it prints, so its value is cross-sectional/forensic, not a fast timing signal.
- **Pre-Reg SHO, Boni (2006) found 42% of listed and 47% of unlisted U.S. stocks had persistent fails of 5+ days, concentrated in expensive-to-borrow names — evidence market makers 'strategically fail' when borrow costs exceed the cost of failing.**  
  Establishes the base mechanism: failing is an economic choice driven by borrow cost, not an anomaly — the through-line connecting every episode from 2003 to 2024.
- **Evans, Geczy, Musto & Reed (2009, Review of Financial Studies) showed a major options market maker, exempt from the locate/borrow rule, chose to FAIL rather than borrow in most hard-to-borrow situations; when failing is cheaper than borrowing, the link between borrow cost and option prices weakens.**  
  Documents the specific legal channel — the options-market-maker exception — that let synthetic short exposure accumulate as fails rather than borrows. This exception was later narrowed and is the target of 2025 reform petitions.
- **Overstock (OSTK) spent more trading days on the Reg SHO threshold list than any other company; CEO Patrick Byrne's decade-long naked-shorting campaign ended with prime-broker settlements — Merrill Lynch Professional Clearing ('Merrill Pro') paid $20M in Jan 2016, and a separate NJ RICO action against Goldman Sachs settled.**  
  The archetypal single-name FTD saga: persistent threshold-list membership plus litigation, but note the stock fell over the crusade years — chronic fails coincided with a declining, not squeezing, price.
- **SEC v. optionsXpress (admin proceeding, charged 2012, ALJ ruling 2013): the firm and customer Jonathan Feldman ran sham 'reset' transactions (buy-writes / deep-in-the-money married puts) from ~Oct 2008 to Mar 2010 to perpetually re-open and disguise multi-million-share FTDs in hard-to-borrow, high-dividend names, evading Rule 204 close-out.**  
  The canonical enforcement case for FTD 'kiting' and dividend-arbitrage fails — reset trades let fails persist indefinitely, and it is why ex-dividend dates in hard-to-borrow names historically show FTD/borrow-fee spikes.
- **GameStop (GME) short interest hit ~122% of float in Jan 2021; SEC/Bloomberg data show ~$359M of GME shares (>1M shares) failed to deliver around Jan 28, 2021, the peak. The SEC's Oct 2021 staff report concluded 'positive sentiment, not buying-to-cover' sustained the run, and that buy-to-cover was 'a small fraction' of volume.**  
  The FTD spike was real and coincided with the price peak, but the regulator attributed the move to directional buying, not a mechanical squeeze — a distinction an Ad Hoc Academic Committee (CLS Blue Sky, 2022) disputed, arguing the SEC ignored securities-lending data.
- **XRT (SPDR S&P Retail ETF) became the classic 'backdoor short' channel: the SEC staff report flagged a spike of ~6M shares of net XRT redemptions on Jan 27, 2021 'consistent with short selling'; XRT short interest has repeatedly exceeded 100% of shares outstanding and it has one of the longest cumulative tenures on the threshold list (reportedly ~1,600+ days; FTDs reported up to ~$400M).**  
  Shorting a basket ETF holding GME (create-to-lend / short-the-ETF) let traders build GME-adjacent short exposure that surfaces as ETF FTDs rather than single-name short interest — the mechanism behind 'hidden short interest' claims.
- **Hard-to-borrow ETFs (XRT retail, IWM small-cap, TLT long Treasuries) recur on threshold lists because AP creation/redemption plus operational settlement lags let fails build in the basket; ETF fails are structurally different from single-name fails because an AP can create real shares to cure them.**  
  ETF FTDs are usually a settlement-plumbing artifact curable by creation, not evidence of a cornered float — a key reason ETF threshold-list appearances rarely precede squeezes the way single-name ones can.
- **MMTLP: FINRA halted trading on Dec 9, 2022 (Rule 6440(a)(3), 'extraordinary event') ahead of the Next Bridge spin-off. Despite mass naked-short claims, FINRA reported FTDs were only 215,238 shares on Dec 9 and estimated short interest ~2.65M shares (~1.6% of shares out); it found no evidence of significant naked shorting.**  
  A high-profile case where the FTD/short data flatly contradicted the retail 'phantom shares' narrative — a caution against inferring naked shorting from price action or halts alone.
- **Trump Media (DJT) appeared on the Nasdaq Reg SHO threshold list around April 17, 2024; CEO Devin Nunes wrote Nasdaq and House chairs alleging naked shorting and naming market makers (Citadel Securities, Virtu, G1, Jane Street). No regulator has substantiated naked shorting; DJT remained volatile and later trended down.**  
  The most recent single-name FTD/threshold-list controversy — again shows threshold-list membership is routinely conflated with illegality, while the SEC maintains fails have many benign causes.
- **Fotak, Raman & Yadav (2014, Journal of Financial Economics): across 1,492 NYSE stocks (2005-2008), higher FTDs were associated with GREATER liquidity and pricing efficiency, no evidence FTDs caused price distortions or 2008 bank failures, and the 2008 pre-borrow order period showed LOWER liquidity and higher pricing errors.**  
  The strongest academic pushback on the naked-short-harm narrative: on average FTDs proxy for market-making activity that aids price discovery, which is why the tradeable edge is in extremes/persistence, not FTDs per se.
- **2008-09 Reg SHO tightening: Rule 204 (interim 204T Sept 18, 2008; final 2009) requires clearing participants to close out short-sale fails by the start of trading on the settlement day after the fail (roughly T+1 after settlement), replacing the old Rule 203(b)(3) 13-consecutive-settlement-day close-out for threshold securities, and removed the options-market-maker close-out exception.**  
  Explains why chronic multi-hundred-day threshold-list tenures (Overstock-style) largely predate 2009, and why post-2009 fails are shorter-lived and more often ETF/operational — a structural break to respect in any historical FTD study.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Threshold-list + hard-borrow squeeze screen* — Small/mid-cap newly added to a Reg SHO SRO threshold list (daily, free) AND short interest >20% of float AND borrow fee >20% annualized AND low days-to-cover-adjusted float → Long equity or long calls in the single name; size small (long / event, days to a few weeks). Persistent threshold-list membership plus genuinely constrained borrow is the fact-pattern that preceded the sharpest single-name spikes (GME Jan 2021). The threshold list is real-time; use borrow/SI (not the lagged FTD number) as the fast trigger.
- *Persistent-FTD overvaluation short* — Names with FTDs that stay >0.5% of shares out for many consecutive prints AND rich valuation / prior run-up, screened cross-sectionally from the biweekly SEC FTD file → Short equity or long puts, diversified basket to survive squeeze risk (short / long-short, weeks to a few months). Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (2015) link FTDs to short-sale-constrained overvaluation that subsequently reverses; the edge is the mean-reversion of constrained-overvaluation, not the fail itself.
- *Ex-dividend hard-borrow lending / borrow-fee spike* — High-dividend, hard-to-borrow names approaching ex-date, where reset/dividend-arb demand historically spikes FTDs and borrow fees → Lend owned shares into the ex-date fee spike (securities-lending income); or avoid holding short over ex-date (long-short (carry), days around each ex-dividend date). Dividend-arbitrage reset transactions (optionsXpress pattern) predictably bid borrow fees into ex-div in constrained names; lenders capture the fee, and it is calendar-timable.
- *ETF fails = plumbing, fade the squeeze narrative* — A basket ETF (XRT, IWM, TLT) on the threshold list or with SI >100% while its APs can freely create — versus a single-name with a genuinely cornered float → Fade ETF-squeeze narratives via ETF options / NAV-arb spreads; concentrate squeeze bets in single names only (short / relative-value, days to weeks). ETF fails are usually curable by creation, so ETF threshold-list membership rarely produces a true squeeze; Fotak et al. (2014) and the AP-creation mechanism argue against paying up for ETF 'hidden short' stories.
- *Do-not-trade the FTD conspiracy tape* — Retail 'naked short / phantom shares' narrative around a halt or threshold listing (MMTLP-, DJT-style) with no corroborating borrow/SI/FTD magnitude → Avoid or fade forced-squeeze longs; require independent borrow-fee and SI confirmation before any squeeze thesis (event (risk-avoidance), immediate). MMTLP (215k-share FTDs, 1.6% SI) and DJT show threshold-list membership is routinely mistaken for illegality; the base rate of these narratives converting to squeezes is low, and downside (halts, delisting) is severe.

**Caveats:**
- SEC FTD data lags 2-4 weeks and is biweekly, so the raw per-name FTD series is stale for timing; the only near-real-time FTD-derived signal is the SRO threshold list, and even that lags the underlying settlement failures.
- The SEC states plainly that FTDs 'are not evidence of abusive or naked short selling' — benign causes (bona fide market-making, corporate actions, long-sale delivery delays, operational error) dominate the aggregate, so FTD level alone is a weak/false signal (Fotak et al. 2014 find FTDs on average correlate with BETTER liquidity).
- The GME squeeze causation is genuinely contested: the SEC staff report (Oct 2021) attributed the move to positive sentiment/directional buying, while an Ad Hoc Academic Committee (CLS Blue Sky, 2022) argued the SEC ignored securities-lending data and understated squeeze/gamma effects. Do not present either as settled fact.
- The XRT '~1,600+ threshold days' and '~$400M FTD' figures circulate via advocacy/petition sources and blogs, not a clean primary SEC tabulation; I could not fully verify the exact cumulative-day count or dollar peak this session — treat as indicative, not precise. The Jan 27, 2021 ~6M-share XRT net-redemption spike IS from the SEC staff report.
- I did not independently verify specific pre-2008 naked-shorting enforcement cases beyond Overstock and the academic record (e.g., Sedona/Rhino Advisors, Universal Express, CMKM Diamonds are widely cited but not confirmed in this session); several 'naked short victim' issuers were themselves later found to be frauds/shells, so pre-2008 anecdotes should be checked case-by-case.
- I could not locate primary SEC FTD/threshold-list figures specific to Sears (SHLD) for 2017-2018; Sears was heavily and legitimately shorted (very high SI, deteriorating fundamentals) into its Oct 2018 Chapter 11, and any 'FTD-central' framing for Sears is unverified here — I flag it rather than assert it.
- The exact optionsXpress monetary sanctions (disgorgement/penalty amounts) I did not pin to a primary figure; the liability finding of sham reset transactions and Rule 204 violations is verified, the dollar amounts are not.
- Threshold-list / borrow-based squeeze longs carry severe tail risk: trading halts (MMTLP), delisting, borrow-recall, and violent reversals; the historical hit rate of 'FTD squeeze' theses is low outside a handful of episodes, and survivorship bias inflates the apparent pattern.
- T+35 FTD 'cycle' claims (Pastorek et al.) are from working papers on a single stock (GME) and are not robustly established; I would not trade a mechanical T+35 timing rule on that basis.
- Regulatory regime shifts matter: Rule 204 (2009) sharply shortened close-out windows and removed the options-MM close-out exception, so chronic multi-hundred-day fails are largely a pre-2009 phenomenon; back-tests spanning the break are non-stationary.

## 8. The skeptic case: why naive FTD strategies disappoint

- **The SEC publishes FTD data only twice a month with a long lag: the first half of a month is posted at end of that month, the second half at ~the 15th of the following month. A fail occurring early in a month is not public for roughly 3-4 weeks.**  
  Any 'live' FTD strategy is trading on data that is stale by weeks. By the time a T+X fail is visible, the borrow tightness, price move, and close-out that created it have already resolved. Timeliness — the core requirement for a tradeable signal — is structurally absent.
- **FTDs are transient by rule. Reg SHO Rule 204 forces close-out of most short-sale fails by the open of the day after settlement (now T+1 settlement since May 28, 2024); only specific deemed-to-own/long-sale and certain AP scenarios get up to T+35. Failure triggers the 'penalty box' (pre-borrow requirement).**  
  A large published FTD is a snapshot of a position the rules are already forcing to unwind, not a durable overhang. The file also cannot age fails — the SEC states the number 'may have little or no relationship to yesterday's aggregate fails,' so you cannot tell a fresh fail from a persistent one.
- **FTDs are tiny and noisy relative to the market. After Rule 204T (Sept 2008) average daily fails fell ~56.6% (from ~1.1 billion to ~478 million shares per GAO/SEC), and SEC OEA reported declines of ~63% across all securities and ~80% for threshold stocks. Total failing shares are a small fraction of volume/market cap. The SEC states fails 'are not necessarily the result of short selling, and are not evidence of abusive short selling or naked short selling.'**  
  The magnitude of the phenomenon was cut in half by one rule, so pre-2009 backtests are non-stationary. A single-day FTD is operational reconciliation noise; only a persistent series carries any borrow-tightness information, and the raw published file does not distinguish the two.
- **The file cannot separate naked shorting from benign fails. ETFs are ~10% of equity market cap but ~78% of FTDs (Evans, Moussawi, Pagano, Sedunov), driven by legitimate 'operational shorting' via the authorized-participant creation/redemption exemption. Separately, the options market-maker exemption lets MMs rationally fail rather than borrow when a name is hard to borrow (Evans, Geczy, Musto, Reed, RFS 2009).**  
  Most FTDs by volume are legal liquidity-provision and MM plumbing, not naked shorting of squeeze candidates. A naive strategy that reads any elevated FTD as 'abusive naked shorting → imminent squeeze' is mostly picking up ETF/market-maker mechanics, not predatory positioning.
- **Published FTD numbers can be masked. In an SEC administrative action (Apr 16, 2012), optionsXpress was found to have used sham 'reset' transactions (married-put/buy-write pairs certain to be exercised) that created the illusion of closing out fails while evading Rule 204's buy-in.**  
  The observable FTD level is a partly manipulable/resettable quantity, not a clean census of true undelivered short exposure. A signal that can be cosmetically reset by the very actors it is supposed to detect is a weak basis for a systematic strategy.
- **The academic event-study evidence is mixed and points against the naive 'fails drive prices down / predict crashes' thesis. Fotak, Raman & Yadav (JFE 2014, ~1,492 NYSE stocks, 2005-2008) find higher FTDs are associated with HIGHER liquidity and pricing efficiency and find no evidence FTDs caused price distortions or firm failures in 2008. Boulton & Braga-Alves (JPM 2012) find returns are positive both before and after surges in naked shorting/persistent fails.**  
  This inverts the retail intuition. If returns are already rising before fails spike, FTDs look like a symptom of pre-existing hard-to-borrow / short-demand conditions rather than a leading cause of decline. The 'fails kill the stock' story lacks robust empirical support.
- **The one predictive FTD result is explicitly un-arbitrageable. Autore, Boulton & Braga-Alves (Financial Review 2015) find threshold-FTD stocks become overvalued with subsequent reversals (overvaluation hypothesis), but note the mispricing 'is likely to be difficult to arbitrage because of extreme shorting costs.'**  
  The condition that generates high persistent fails (hard-to-borrow, high borrow fees, low float) is exactly the condition that makes shorting the overvaluation prohibitively expensive and squeeze-prone. The signal that 'works' on paper is the one you cannot cheaply trade net of borrow costs and short-squeeze tail risk.
- **The 'phantom shares / MOASS / infinite squeeze via naked shorting' narrative is contradicted by the regulator's forensic review of its canonical case. The SEC Staff Report on early-2021 markets (Oct 18, 2021) found GameStop did NOT experience persistent FTDs during the January 2021 run-up, found no evidence a short squeeze or gamma squeeze caused the price rise ('it was positive sentiment, not buying-to-cover, that sustained' the appreciation), and explained short interest >100% via legitimate re-lending of the same shares, not counterfeit/phantom shares.**  
  The most-cited real-world 'proof' of an FTD-driven infinite squeeze, on examination, showed low fails and a sentiment-driven (not mechanics-driven) move. Trading the MOASS thesis means betting on a mechanism the SEC's own data-level review did not find in the flagship example.

**Tradeable angles surfaced here:**
- *Fade the FTD/MOASS narrative spike (do not buy the squeeze thesis)* — Viral 'T+35 cycle / FTD spike → imminent squeeze' chatter coinciding with a parabolic move in a low-float, heavily-promoted name, where the actual published fails are elevated but NOT persistent across weeks. → Defined-risk options (long puts or put spreads) on the hyped equity; avoid outright short stock. (short (via defined-risk options), Days to a few weeks (post blow-off)). SEC's GME review and Fotak/Boulton evidence indicate fails do not cause the up-move (sentiment does) and reversals follow overvaluation; but borrow cost and squeeze risk are extreme, so any expression must be capped-loss. This is more 'fade the mechanism claim' than a clean alpha source.
- *Persistent-fail overvaluation short filter (mostly a non-trade)* — Reg SHO threshold-security-list membership (aggregate fails >=10,000 shares AND >=0.5% of shares outstanding for >=5 consecutive settlement days) combined with an independently high, rising borrow fee. → Long puts where listed and liquid; borrow-cost screen as gate. (short, Weeks to a few months). Autore/Boulton/Braga-Alves (2015) find threshold-fail stocks are overvalued with reversals — but they explicitly flag it is hard to arbitrage due to extreme shorting costs. The honest conclusion is that once borrow cost and squeeze tail risk are netted, this is largely un-tradeable; it belongs here as a documented effect that fails as a strategy.
- *Use FTDs only as a confirming filter, never a standalone trigger* — Require (a) fail persistence over multiple SEC releases, (b) corroboration from a separate real-time source (borrow fee/utilization, short interest, price/volume), and (c) exclusion of ETFs and heavily-optioned names where fails are dominated by operational/MM shorting. → N/A — a risk/entry filter layered on an existing thesis, not a position itself. (long-short, N/A (overlay)). Given publication lag, noise, resettability, and attribution failure, the defensible use of the file is to veto/confirm — e.g., discount a 'naked shorting' thesis when the fails are ETF operational shorting or MM exemption artifacts — rather than to generate signals. Reduces false positives more than it creates edge.

**Caveats:**
- Publication lag makes any real-time FTD strategy impossible in principle: the SEC file is released twice a month with a multi-week delay, so live 'FTD signals' are always stale.
- The academic evidence is genuinely mixed and sample/period/specification dependent: Fotak/Raman/Yadav and Boulton/Braga-Alves find no predictable decline (and positive pre/post returns), while Autore/Boulton/Braga-Alves find overvaluation reversal — the sign of the effect is not stable across studies, which itself argues against a robust naive strategy.
- The one directional (overvaluation-reversal) result is characterized by its own authors as difficult to arbitrage due to extreme shorting costs, so 'statistically real' does not imply 'tradeable net of borrow cost and squeeze risk.'
- The 'edge decay from widespread scraping' claim is a reasoned market-efficiency argument plus documented regulatory regime shifts (Rule 203/204, T+2→T+1); I did not find a published study that specifically measures alpha decay of an FTD strategy over time, so treat that particular point as inference, not measured fact.
- Magnitude figures such as fails being a tiny fraction of volume/market cap and the ~56.6% post-Rule 204T decline come from SEC/GAO memos and secondary summaries; treat them as order-of-magnitude and regime-specific rather than current constants.
- The Reg SHO threshold definition (>=10,000 shares and >=0.5% of shares outstanding for 5 consecutive settlement days) and the T+35 vs T+1 close-out timing are stated from the regulatory framework; exact applicability varies by transaction type (short sale vs deemed-to-own long sale vs AP/MM), and I did not re-verify every sub-clause line-by-line.
- The optionsXpress matter established that reset transactions can create sham close-outs; note a separately-charged customer fraud count was later dismissed, so the load-bearing takeaway is narrowly the reset/close-out mechanism, not a broad fraud finding.

## Compliance note

Trading on FTD data is legal — it is public SEC data, and the strategies above express views through ordinary instruments. Two lines matter, and the catalog flags them inline:

- **Detecting** manipulative patterns (sham "reset" transactions, faked locates, abusive naked shorting) is legitimate analysis. **Executing** them — reset trades, deep-ITM buy-writes / married puts structured to fake a close-out, false bona-fide-market-making claims to evade Reg SHO Rules 203/204 — is illegal (see the SEC's optionsXpress action). The reset-detection and options-borrow strategies are written as detection/relative-value, not as instructions to run resets.
- Micro-cap and hard-to-borrow names, where fails concentrate, carry acute manipulation, halt, and liquidity risk. Position sizing and defined-risk expression are assumed throughout.

None of this is investment advice, and none of these signals has been backtested here.

- ○ **IOSCO 'Findings during COVID-19' (PD682) and CNBC/ETF.com reporting, 16 Mar 2020.** — Bond ETFs traded at large discounts to NAV during the March 2020 dash-for-cash (AGG ~-4.4%, BND ~-6.2%, LQD ~-4.5% on 16 Mar 2020) as AP arbitrage broke down — an empirical instance of ETF liquidity mismatch, relieved after the Fed SMCCF backstop.
- ○ **SEC Office of Compliance Inspections and Examinations (2013), 'Risk Alert: Strengthening Practices for Preventing and Detecting Illegal Options Trading Used to Reset Reg SHO Close-out Obligations,' Aug 9, 2013 (sec.gov/about/offices/ocie/options-trading-risk-alert.pdf); companion SEC Press Release 2013-151.** — Names deep-ITM buy-writes/married puts and short-dated FLEX and very-short-dated listed options as tools to fake Rule 204 close-outs; lists detection red flags (large persistent FTDs across multiple HTB/threshold names, low open interest apart from the trader, options expiring just after the purported purchase settles, repeat counterparties, non-bona-fide MM locate claims) and states sham close-outs violate Reg SHO.
- ○ **SEC, In re optionsXpress, Inc., Thomas E. Stern & Jonathan I. Feldman, Commission Opinion, Securities Act Rel. No. 10125 / Exchange Act Rel. No. 78621, Admin. Proc. File No. 3-14848 (Aug 18, 2016); charges announced in SEC Press Release 2012-66 (Apr 16, 2012); ALJ initial decision June 7, 2013.** — Sustained that optionsXpress violated Reg SHO Rules 204/204T through repeated sham 'reset' transactions (Oct 2008-Mar 2010) that gave the illusion of like-kind-and-quantity purchases; imposed disgorgement and a third-tier penalty, but held fraud was not proven against customer Feldman (who traded ~$2.9B buys / ~$1.7B option shorts in 2009).
- ○ **SEC, first naked-short-selling enforcement actions against Hazan Capital Management LLC / Steven M. Hazan and TJM Proprietary Trading LLC (Benson, Burke), SEC Press Release 2009-179 (Aug 5, 2009).** — Charged use of reverse conversions in Reg SHO threshold securities to create the appearance of close-out compliance and to improperly claim the bona-fide-market-maker locate exception; settlements ~ $3.0M (Hazan) and ~$541K (TJM), Rule 203(b) locate/close-out violations.
- ○ **17 CFR 242.204 (Reg SHO Rule 204, close-out requirement) and 242.203(b) (locate requirement and market-maker exception); adopted/amended via SEC Exchange Act Release No. 34-60388 (July 2009).** — Requires closing out short-sale FTDs by borrowing or purchasing like-kind-and-quantity shares by the start of regular trading hours on the settlement day after settlement date (longer for long-sale/bona-fide-MM fails); defines the locate obligation and the MM exemption that reset/locate schemes exploit.
- ○ **Cboe Regulatory Circular RG08-063 (May 19, 2008), 'Regulation SHO (Short Sales) Close-Out Requirement, Use of Option Transactions' (cdn.cboe.com/resources/regulation/circulars/regulatory/RG08-063.pdf); related RG12-022 on Rule 204.** — Exchange-level warning to members that option transactions may not be used to satisfy Reg SHO close-out obligations; part of a cross-SRO body of guidance predating the SEC's 2013 alert.
- ○ **Evans, R., Geczy, C., Musto, D. & Reed, A. (2009), 'Failure Is an Option: Impediments to Short Selling and Options Prices,' Review of Financial Studies 22(5): 1955-1980.** — Using a major options market maker's transactions, shows that in most hard-to-borrow situations the MM chooses to fail rather than borrow, and that the value of failing is partly impounded into option prices — the economic basis for synthetic-cheaper-than-real dislocations.
- ○ **Hao, J., Kalay, A. & Mayhew, S. (2010), 'Ex-Dividend Arbitrage in Option Markets,' Review of Financial Studies 23(1): 271-303.** — Documents that a large fraction of deep-ITM call open interest goes unexercised around ex-dividend dates, enabling a coordinated deep-ITM option 'dividend play' that harvests the windfall and inflates reported option volume — the mechanism behind ex-dividend option/FTD clustering.
- ○ **FINRA, Report on FINRA's Examination and Risk Monitoring Program — Regulation SHO section (2023 report; also 2024 and 2025 Annual Regulatory Oversight Reports), finra.org.** — Cites recurring deficiencies: improper claims of bona-fide market making to obtain locate/close-out exemptions and improper reuse of locates for hard-to-borrow/threshold securities after intraday buy-to-cover — the live exam analogues of reset/manufactured-locate conduct.
- ○ **SEC, Fails-to-Deliver Data (semi-monthly), sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/fails-deliver-data; SRO daily threshold-security lists (threshold = CNS fails >=10,000 shares AND >=0.5% of shares outstanding for 5 consecutive settlement days).** — Public ground-truth data for FTD levels/persistence and threshold membership; aggregate, net-at-CNS, and lagged, so usable for screening but not for attributing conduct to accounts.
- ○ **SEC, 'Shortening the Securities Transaction Settlement Cycle' (Rule 15c6-1/-2 and Reg SHO Rule 204 conforming changes); standard T+1 compliance date May 28, 2024.** — Confirms settlement moved to T+1 in May 2024, compressing every Rule 204 close-out window and thus the timeline any reset scheme must repeatedly beat.
- ○ **U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 'Fails-to-Deliver Data,' sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/fails-deliver-data** — Primary source: pipe-delimited file layout (Settlement Date, CUSIP, Symbol, Quantity(Fails), Description, Price), twice-monthly release schedule, coverage from Feb 2004, the pre/post Sept 16 2008 10,000-share threshold change, sourcing from NSCC CNS aggregated across all members, and the caveats that FTDs are not necessarily short selling/naked shorting and that price may not match other sources.
- ○ **SEC Regulation SHO, 17 CFR 242.203 & 242.204 (Rule 204 adopted in Release No. 34-60388, 2009); Rule 203(b)(3) threshold-security close-out** — Close-out framework: mandatory buy-in of fail positions shortly after settlement, 13-consecutive-settlement-day threshold-security close-out, and the bona-fide market-maker locate exception that permits fails.
- ○ **SEC, 'Shortening the Securities Transaction Settlement Cycle' (final rule, compliance date May 28, 2024), sec.gov settlement-cycle compliance guide** — T+1 transition compressed Rule 204 close-out timeframes; FTD close-out dynamics differ pre- vs post-May-2024 (release number unverified this session).
- ○ **Fotak, V., Raman, V. & Yadav, P. (2014), 'Fails-to-deliver, short selling, and market quality,' Journal of Financial Economics 114(3):493-516** — For ~1,492 NYSE stocks (2005-2008), higher FTDs are associated with higher liquidity and pricing efficiency, with no evidence FTDs caused price distortions or firm failures in 2008 — the benign/skeptic view.
- ○ **Stratmann, T. & Welborn, J. (2016), 'Informed short selling, fails-to-deliver, and abnormal returns,' Journal of Empirical Finance 38(PA):81-102** — Stocks with FTDs experience negative abnormal returns proportional to their FTD levels (event studies and Fama-French portfolios), consistent with FTDs reflecting nonbinding short-sale constraints and informed short selling — the return-predictability view.
- ○ **Autore, D., Boulton, T. & Braga-Alves, M. (2015), 'Failures to Deliver, Short Sale Constraints, and Stock Overvaluation,' Financial Review 50(2):143-172** — Links fails-to-deliver to short-sale constraints and subsequent stock overvaluation/negative returns, supporting the constraint-proxy interpretation of FTD data.
- ○ **Fintel (fintel.io/sftd/), ChartExchange (chartexchange.com/symbol/.../failure-to-deliver/), stockgrid.io, ForcedAlpha (forcedalpha.com/tools/ftd/)** — Vendors re-serve the free SEC FTD file with their own CUSIP-to-ticker mapping and per-symbol charts; value is convenience/mapping, not additional data — NSCC/CNS participant-level data is not available from them.
- ○ **Stratmann, T., & Welborn, J. W. (2016). 'Informed short selling, fails-to-deliver, and abnormal returns.' Journal of Empirical Finance, 38(Part A), 81-102.** — High-FTD stocks earn negative abnormal returns proportional to their FTD levels, consistent with informed short selling; supports the bearish, contrarian-to-the-spike interpretation of FTD signals.
- ○ **Fotak, V., Raman, V., & Yadav, P. K. (2014). 'Fails-to-deliver, short selling, and market quality.' Journal of Financial Economics, 114(3), 493-516.** — Across 1,492 NYSE stocks (2005-2008), greater FTDs associate with higher liquidity and pricing efficiency and show no evidence of causing price distortions or financial-firm failures in 2008 -- the peer-reviewed skeptic anchor against 'FTD = harmful manipulation'.
- ○ **Boni, L. (2006). 'Strategic delivery failures in U.S. equity markets.' Journal of Financial Markets, 9(1), 1-26.** — Pre-Reg-SHO, ~42% of listed and ~47% of unlisted stocks had persistent (5+ day) fails, concentrated in expensive-to-borrow names, consistent with market makers strategically failing when borrow costs are high.
- ○ **Evans, R. B., Moussawi, R., Pagano, M. S., & Sedunov, J. 'ETF Short Interest and Failures-to-Deliver: Naked Short-Selling or Operational Shorting?' (SSRN abstract 2961954 / Jacobs Levy Center working paper).** — ETFs are ~10% of equity market cap but >20% of short interest and ~78% of FTDs, driven by authorized-participant 'operational shorting'; improves liquidity/efficiency but clusters by AP and proxies counterparty/systemic risk -- basis for excluding ETF fails from single-stock signals and for the ETF-FTD risk-monitor angle.
- ○ **McLean, R. D., & Pontiff, J. (2016). 'Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?' Journal of Finance, 71(1), 5-32.** — Cross-sectional anomaly returns fall ~26% out-of-sample and ~58% post-publication; used here to discount the live edge of FTD anomalies documented on 2004-2008 data.
- ○ **Pastorek, D., Drabek, M., & Albrecht, P. (2023). 'Confirmation of T+35 Failures-To-Deliver Cycles: Evidence from GameStop Corp.' Czech Journal of Economics and Finance (Finance a uver), 73(1), 56-80.** — Using wavelet coherence on GameStop-related ETF FTDs, reports consistent ~T+35 cycles and weaker T+3-to-T+6 cycles -- the only peer-reviewed support for T+X FTD cyclicality; single-asset, meme-era, and does not establish net-of-cost price predictability.
